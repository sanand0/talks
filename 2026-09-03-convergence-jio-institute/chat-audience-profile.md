## The audience, psychologically

Putting all five questions together, I see roughly four overlapping mindsets.

**1. The eager delegator.**
“AI, please eliminate the drudgery.” They are already sold on usefulness. They don't need convincing.

**2. The reluctant verifier.**
“I'd delegate much more if checking the output didn't negate the benefit.” This is probably the modal person in the room.

**3. The protective humanist.**
“Automate execution, but judgment/creativity/relationships remain mine.” This is the dominant philosophical position.

**4. The anxious apprentice.**
“If AI does the work through which I would have learned judgment, how do I ever acquire judgment?” Particularly important given the Jio learner cohort.

There is also a small fifth group:

**5. The full delegator.**
“Why retain work at all?” About 13% say nothing meaningful needs to remain human when directly asked.

---

## The deepest pattern I see

There are actually **three layers of work** in their answers:

**Layer 1 — Friction:** gathering, cleaning, emailing, scheduling, summarizing, formatting.
→ _Please automate nearly all of this._

**Layer 2 — Production:** analysis, coding, drafting, reporting, research.
→ _Increasingly comfortable automating this, provided it can be verified._

**Layer 3 — Agency:** deciding what's worth doing, what is true enough, which trade-off matters, whom to trust, who is accountable.
→ _They overwhelmingly still claim this for themselves._

And their unresolved anxiety is:

**If Layer 2 is how humans historically learned Layer 3, what happens when AI removes Layer 2?**

I think that's the most interesting thing the survey is telling you about this room. It ties together the student career questions, senior managers' judgment concerns, hallucination/verification questions, and the repeated desire to retain framing and decision-making.
