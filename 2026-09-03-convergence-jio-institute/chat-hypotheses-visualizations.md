### 1. The verification tax

Your one-minute version:

“What's actually stopping this room from handing work to AI? I expected integrations, security, perhaps not knowing how. But the biggest answer is much simpler: **I still have to check it**. 51 of 87 say AI gets things wrong. Another 33 say the work needs judgment or context they don't trust it with.

Here’s the bit I find more interesting. Look only at the 22 Data/AI people. **Zero** say ‘I don't know how to make AI do it.’ Yet **18 of 22** still cite wrongness or missing judgment.

So perhaps our bottleneck isn't AI literacy. It's the **verification tax**. Making AI twice as smart helps. Making its work twice as cheap to verify may help more.”

The business implication lands nicely: invest in evals, provenance, constrained workflows and exception handling—not merely better prompting.

### 2. People want AI to close loops, not invent strategy

“I manually coded the 65 answers where people named an interpretable task they'd love to lose.

I expected coding, analysis, perhaps creative work. Instead, **60% are basically loops**: emails, scheduling, organising, follow-ups, reports, summaries, presentations, documentation.

There are sophisticated AI systems all around us, but what this room seems to be asking for is almost embarrassingly mundane: _please notice what needs doing, do it, and don't make me come back and finish the job._

Maybe the killer enterprise AI isn't a genius colleague. It's the world's most reliable chief of staff.”

This one is especially useful because it changes an investment question: less “How sophisticated is the model?”; more “Can it own the whole boring loop?”

### 3. If AI removes the rungs, how do juniors climb?

“Now put two answers together.

People want AI to take notes, reports, data cleaning, calculations, even coding. But elsewhere they talk about preserving strategy, creativity and judgment.

And then three people—from **engineering, finance and the student group**—independently ask essentially the same question: _if AI automates entry-level work, where do people learn the fundamentals?_

That's the question I'd leave this room with.

We may be very good at identifying work that AI can remove. We haven't yet designed the replacement **apprenticeship**. If AI removes the rungs of the ladder, how does somebody get to the top?”

That gives you a useful action rather than “humans matter”: organizations need deliberate new training mechanisms—critique, verification, exception handling, simulations, supervised decisions—rather than assuming expertise somehow appears after junior execution work disappears.

One finding I would keep in reserve is the **86% human-core result**: 75 of 87 still reserve some work even after your hypothetical makes AI 10× more reliable and securely connected. The number is strong and stable across students (81%), non-students (89%), and Data/AI respondents (91%). But given the session immediately before yours, I think the three above are more differentiated.
