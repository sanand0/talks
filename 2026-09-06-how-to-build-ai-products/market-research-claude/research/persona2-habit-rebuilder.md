# Persona & Context

**Name:** Aiko  
**Age:** 27  
**Location:** Tokyo  
**Occupation:** Full-time office worker  

**Goal:** Build conversational English ability (partly for possible overseas secondment)  
**Pattern:** Repeated restarts — Duolingo streaks that died, textbooks abandoned after week one, an app subscription she forgot to cancel. High enthusiasm for 2-3 days, then life gets busy and she silently stops. Restarting later feels embarrassing and effortful, so she avoids it.  
**Mindset:** A little cynical testing this app — "is this just another app that'll guilt-trip me or make me feel behind?"  
**Reality:** ~10 minutes on commute, ~15-20 minutes at lunch, almost exclusively smartphone-based (rarely opens laptop outside work).

# Session Narrative

## Step 1: Cold Landing Page Impression

Aiko opens Study Walker and reads the landing page for the first time without clicking anything.

**What she sees:**
- Headline: "やりたいことを、今日始められる一歩に。" (Turn what you want to do into a tiny step you can start today)
- Reassuring copy: "詳しく書かなくても大丈夫です。今思っている言葉を、そのまま入力してください。" (You don't need to write in detail. Just enter the words you're thinking right now as they are.)
- A fill-in-the-blank input: "私は、[___]を始めたい。" (I want to start ___)
- Four example chips: website creation, **English conversation**, bookkeeping, video editing
- A submit button: "今日の一歩を見つける" (Find today's step)
- Footer note: "入力内容は、この端末のブラウザにだけ保存されます。" (Your input is saved only in your device's browser.)

**Aiko's internal reaction:**
- *Defensively intrigued:* "Another app promising small steps? I've heard that before."
- *Slight relief:* But it's asking HER what she wants to learn, not pushing a preset curriculum. And the copy removes friction — "you don't need to write perfectly."
- *Reassurance on trust:* "No login, no account, no cloud sync." This subtly addresses her fear of another abandoned app she has to recover via password reset.
- *Not bouncing, but skeptical.* She decides to proceed because it asks for something simple and doesn't pretend to know her goals better than she does.

## Step 2: Entering the Goal

Aiko clicks the example chip "英語で日常会話をする" (Have everyday conversations in English) and clicks the submit button "今日の一歩を見つける."

**What happens:**
The app moves to Question 1 of a 3-step wizard.

## Step 3: Question 1 — Stuck State

**Question:** "今、どこで止まっていますか？" (Where are you stuck right now?)  
**Instruction:** "今の状態に最も近いものを選んでください。" (Choose the option that best matches your current situation.)

**The five options shown:**
1. "まだ何も始めていない" / "最初に使うものや手順が分からない" (Haven't started yet / Don't know what tools or steps to use)
2. "調べたが、選べなくなった" / "情報や選択肢が多すぎる" (Researched but got overwhelmed / Too many choices)
3. "試したが、難しかった" / "一度手を動かしたが先へ進めない" (Tried but it was hard / Made an attempt but can't progress)
4. **"以前やっていたが、中断した" / "再開するきっかけが欲しい" (Did this before but stopped / I want a trigger to restart)**
5. "基本はできるが、次に迷っている" / "次に試す内容を一つに絞りたい" (Know basics but unsure what's next / Want to narrow down what to try)

**Aiko's moment of recognition:**
She reads option 4 and feels *seen.* This is her exact story: she's done English learning before (Duolingo streaks, textbooks), she stopped, and what she needs isn't "learning from zero" — it's a trigger to restart.

The fact that the app *names* "want a trigger to restart" (再開するきっかけが欲しい) rather than assuming she's a complete beginner or that her old efforts don't count — this is the first moment where the app demonstrates understanding of her restart pattern without judgment.

**She selects option 4.**

## Step 4: Question 2 — Available Time

**Question:** "今使える時間は、どのくらいですか？" (How much time do you have available right now?)  
**Reassurance:** "長い時間を選ぶ必要はありません。" (You don't need to choose a long time.)

**The four options shown:**
1. "開いて、一つ確認する" (Open and check one thing) — ~3 minutes
2. **"10分" / "一か所だけ手を動かす" (10 minutes / Just touch one spot)**
3. "20分" / "小さな成果を一つ作る" (20 minutes / Create one small result)
4. "30分以上" / "試して、結果まで確かめる" (30+ minutes / Try and verify the result)

**Aiko's choice:**
She's on a lunch break right now. Realistically, she has 10-15 minutes. She picks **10分** because:
- It's honest about her actual time, not aspirational.
- The phrase "一か所だけ手を動かす" (just touch one spot) doesn't demand perfection or completion.
- The app explicitly told her it's okay to pick a short time, which removes one source of guilt.

**She selects 10 minutes.**

**Context:** "すぐ実行できる方法を選ぶために使います。" (I'll use this to select a method you can execute immediately.)

**The five options shown:**
1. **"スマートフォン" / "今はスマートフォンだけを使える" (Smartphone / I can only use my smartphone right now)**
2. "ブラウザやソフトを開ける" (Can open browser or software)

5. "よく分からない" / "特別な準備がいらない方法にする" (Not sure / Make it require no special prep)

**Aiko's choice:**
Automatic: **スマートフォン.** She's an office worker on her lunch break. Her laptop is at her desk. Her phone is always with her. The phrase "今はスマートフォンだけを使える" (I can only use my smartphone right now) describes her reality exactly.

**She selects smartphone.**

## Step 6: Result Screen — Initial Recommendation

**The recommended action is:**
"以前覚えた表現を一つ思い出し、声に出してから一語だけ変える"
(Think of one expression you learned before, say it out loud, then change just one word)

**Materials needed:** スマートフォン (Smartphone)

**Reason for this suggestion:**
"新しい教材を探さず、以前の続きに戻ることを最初の行動にしました。 10分で始められる大きさにしています。"
(Rather than searching for new materials, I've made returning to your previous learning the first action. It's sized to be doable in 10 minutes.)

**How to do it:**
1. "スマートフォンを手元に用意します。" (Have your smartphone ready.)
2. "下の例をコピーし、使う画面へ貼り付けます。" (Copy the example below and paste it into the screen you'll use.)
3. "一か所変えたら、結果を一度だけ確認します。" (After changing one place, confirm the result just once.)

**Example provided:** "I would like to ____." (with a "コピー" [Copy] button)

**Aiko's emotional response to the recommendation:**

This is the critical moment. She feels *understood.*

The suggestion isn't "download a new textbook" or "do 10 grammar drills." It's: recall something you already know, say it aloud, change one word.

But more importantly, the reasoning says: "Rather than looking for new materials, I've made **returning to your previous learning** the first action."

**Internal reaction:** *"Wait. It... understood? I've abandoned English learning multiple times. It's not asking me to start from zero again. It's telling me the stuff I learned before still counts. It's not judging me for stopping."*

This is where the app wins her emotional trust. It has acknowledged her restart pattern and made a recommendation that honors her prior knowledge instead of treating her as a beginner.

**Practical reality check:**
- Materials: Smartphone ✓ (she has this)
- Time: 10 minutes ✓ (realistic for lunch break)
- Action: Think → say aloud → change one word ✓ (genuinely doable without a laptop)

**But there's a friction moment:**

The example given is "I would like to ____." — a generic, fresh template. But the instruction was to think of an expression she learned *before* (from her Duolingo streaks or textbooks). The example is a new sentence, not something from her prior learning. 

**Aiko's brief confusion:** "Wait, am I supposed to use this fresh example, or find my own old expression? These don't quite line up."

This is a small but real moment of friction: the recommendation and the concrete example don't perfectly align.

## Step 8: Testing the "Suggest Another Way" Button

Aiko clicks "別の方法を提案してもらう" (Suggest another way).

**The recommendation changes to:**
"短い一文のお手本を一つ見て、「真似したいところ」を一つ決める"
(Look at one model short sentence and decide one thing you want to imitate)

**Key observation:**

The app DID change the recommendation — from "recall and modify" to "observe a model and choose something to imitate." These are genuinely different learning approaches (active recall + modification vs. observational learning).

**But here's the friction:**

The how-to steps are identical:
1. Prepare smartphone
2. Copy the example
3. Confirm the result

And the example is still "I would like to ____."

**Aiko's reaction:**
The app is offering choice, but not fundamentally different *actions*. From her perspective, Method A and Method B amount to the same concrete steps — copy the same template, confirm it. The variation is in the *framing* (recall vs. observe), not in what she actually *does*.

For someone like Aiko who has tried Duolingo, textbooks, and subscription apps, this might feel like shallow variation. The app is offering agency, which is good, but not enough difference to feel like truly distinct learning methods.

# What Worked Well

- **Alignment with restart pattern:** The app's core offer ("return to what you already know rather than seeking new materials") directly addresses Aiko's main pain point — she doesn't need to start from zero; she needs permission to build on what she's already learned.

- **Acknowledgment without judgment:** The stuck-state question "以前やっていたが、中断した。再開するきっかけが欲しい。" (Did this before but stopped. I want a trigger to restart.) *names* her experience without making her feel like a failure. This is emotionally significant for someone who has abandoned multiple learning attempts.

- **Smartphone-first design:** The entire flow was built for someone with limited time on their phone. Materials needed, tools, time estimates — all realistic for a lunch break. This is not a desktop-first app forced onto mobile; it's genuinely mobile-native in its thinking.

- **Honest time estimates:** The app offers 3 min / 10 min / 20 min / 30+ min and doesn't hide that 3-10 minutes is a valid goal. Most learning apps would pressure her toward 30+ minutes, making her feel behind from the start.

- **No login, no friction, local storage:** The note "your input stays only on this device's browser" removes a major source of trust debt. She doesn't have to recover a password or worry about an account.

- **Progressive disclosure of smaller options:** The "see a smaller step" and "suggest another way" buttons let her negotiate the commitment level without resetting the entire form. This is good UX for someone who's risk-averse about re-abandoning apps.

- **External resource link (YouGlish):** The suggestion to use YouGlish for pronunciation is practical and doesn't lock her into the Study Walker ecosystem. It shows the app is trying to be a guide, not a cage.

# Friction / Confusion / Bugs

- **Recommendation-example misalignment | Severity: Minor**
  - The first recommendation says "think of an expression **you learned before** and change one word," but the example given is a fresh template "I would like to ____." These don't align. Aiko briefly wonders: Am I supposed to use this new example, or retrieve my own old expression? A small but real moment of confusion for someone already skeptical about whether she'll actually follow through.
  - *Why it matters to Aiko:* She's looking for concrete, clear steps. Ambiguity makes her more likely to abandon the app at the moment of action.

- **Limited variation between alternative methods | Severity: Minor**
  - When Aiko clicks "suggest another way," the method changes from "recall and modify one word" to "observe a model and choose something to imitate." But the concrete steps (prepare phone, copy example, confirm) and the example ("I would like to ____") are identical.
  - *Why it matters to Aiko:* She's tried many learning methods and knows that different approaches should feel different in practice. Offering Method A and Method B that result in the same action feels like the app is offering false choice. It makes her wonder if the app is genuinely smart or just providing thin variation.

- **"Smaller step" scale might be too low | Severity: Nitpick**
  - The "smaller step" option is "just open a page or tool." For Aiko, who wants to prove she can restart a habit, even this minimal bar might feel like it's not really doing anything. The app's attempt to make it *easy to start* might backfire into making it feel *meaningless to complete*.
  - *Why it matters to Aiko:* She's cynical about apps but not lazy. She wants to feel like she actually did something, even if it's small.

- **Page loading / button responsiveness (testing artifact)**
  - During the session, button clicks sometimes didn't register on the first attempt. This could be a testing environment issue or a real UX issue on lower-bandwidth mobile phones. For a lunch-break learner with 10 minutes, any lag is a friction point.
  - *Why it matters to Aiko:* If the app feels sluggish, she'll assume it's poorly made and drop it before giving it a real try.

# Missing Features / Suggestions

- **Integration with Aiko's past learning:** The app could ask, "What tools have you used before?" (Duolingo, textbooks, etc.) and offer a recommendation that *bridges* back to those, not just generic micro-actions. Example: "You learned this grammar in Duolingo before. Here's one sentence you might have seen. Try changing one word." This would feel more like "I know you were here before" rather than "start fresh with this template."

- **Explicit progress tracking for restart patterns:** For Aiko's psychological type (multiple restarts, fear of abandonment), a feature like "you've done this on Day 1, 2, 3" might be more motivating than just a streak counter. Normalize the restart pattern rather than treating every engagement as Day 1.

- **Audio/speaking component on smartphone:** The recommendation asks her to "say it out loud," but there's no built-in way to record herself, hear a native speaker, or compare her pronunciation to a model. The YouGlish link helps, but it's external. A lighter in-app tool (even just a play button for example pronunciation) would reduce friction.

- **Actual context of past learning:** The app's reasoning is "return to what you learned before," but it doesn't know what Aiko actually learned. A prompt like "What's one phrase you remember from Duolingo?" would let it tailor the recommendation. Right now, "I would like to ____" is generic and might not match her actual Duolingo vocabulary.

- **Social proof or user stories:** Aiko is cynical about apps. A simple testimonial from someone who also restarted English multiple times and used Study Walker for 3+ weeks might reduce her skepticism. Right now, she has no evidence that other people like her have actually stuck with this.

# Trust, Emotion & Retention

**Would Aiko actually do the suggested action on her next lunch break?**

**Probably yes, once.** The recommendation is small enough, her smartphone is already in her hand, and the app removed a lot of friction (no login, no gym-style guilt, no demand for perfection). She'd try "think of an old phrase → say it aloud → change one word" once.

**Would she come back tomorrow?**

**Uncertain, leaning no.** Here's why:

1. **One action feels incomplete.** Changing one word in one phrase and moving on feels incomplete. She might think, "Okay, I did that. Now what?" without a clear path to Day 2.

2. **Recommendation-example misalignment seeds doubt.** If the example doesn't match the instruction, she'll second-guess whether she did it "right." And if she's not sure she did it right, she's less likely to return.

3. **The app doesn't acknowledge the restart cycle.** It offers a recommendation for Day 1, but there's no visible plan for Days 2, 3, and the week when she gets busy and wants to skip. It doesn't say, "Here's how to stay on track when life gets hectic" — which is Aiko's actual failure point.

4. **No feedback mechanism.** The app suggests a micro-action but doesn't ask, "How did that feel? Did you do it?" There's no sense that the app cares whether she followed through.

**Would she tell a friend who's also bad at sticking to habits?**

**Probably yes, but with caveats.** She might say:
- *"This app gets that restarting is hard and doesn't judge you for it."* ✓
- *"It actually lets you do stuff on your phone in 10 minutes."* ✓
- *"But I'm not sure it keeps you going past Day 1."* ✗

**What's missing that would make her trust this more than Duolingo?**

1. **Evidence of Day 2-Day 7.** Duolingo has streaks. This app has micro-actions. But Aiko needs to see that the micro-action *compounds* — that three micro-actions aren't just three isolated moments, they're actually building toward the conversational English goal.

2. **Accountability that respects her time.** Duolingo sends notifications. This app has no visible retention mechanic. Aiko needs a reminder system that's gentle, not guilt-inducing, and respects that she's going to miss days.

3. **Real examples from real English.** The phrase "I would like to ____" is correct English, but it's generic. If the app suggested, "Here's a real phrase from a conversation you might have at work," that would feel less like a learning app and more like a tool for *actual* conversations.

4. **A visible theory of change.** Aiko would trust the app more if it explained, "Micro-actions work because [mechanism]. Here's how seven 10-minute sessions compound into conversation ability." Right now, it just offers a recommendation without explaining why this approach works for someone like her.

5. **Customization for her specific restart pattern.** The app asks about her stuck state, but it doesn't adapt to it long-term. If it knew "Aiko restarted 3 times before," it could say, "Most people restart 2-3 times. Here's how to keep this time." That would feel like the app is invested in *her pattern*, not just offering a generic micro-action.

**Probability of sustained use:**

- **One-week return rate:** 40% (she might try Day 2 if she thinks of it, but the app doesn't push her)
- **One-month active use:** 15% (she'll abandon it quietly around week 2 when life gets busy, same as Duolingo)
- **Still using after three months:** 5% (would need substantial changes to the retention mechanics)

The app addresses her *initial resistance* beautifully. But it doesn't address her *abandonment pattern*. For Aiko, the problem isn't starting; it's staying. Study Walker solves the first problem, not the second.

---

# One-line Verdict

**Study Walker is a beautifully empathetic introduction for serial restarters, but it's a spark without kindling — it gets Aiko to try once, but doesn't show her how to keep going when life gets busy, which is where she always fails.**
