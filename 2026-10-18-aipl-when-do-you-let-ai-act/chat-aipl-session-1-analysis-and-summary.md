**It gets 95 right.**

How much would you let it do?

**Version B**

An AI checks 100 expense claims and decides which can be paid.

**It gets 5 wrong.**

How much would you let it do?

Ten of 28 respondents wrote “Nothing” or “None.” That does **not** establish that they believe AI can do everything; it may simply mean they did not encounter a task they considered unsuitable last week.

### Q4. People select checks by impact, but impact alone is incomplete

Alongside the 20 who choose the costliest potential mistakes, nine of 32 say they would not use AI if limited to ten checks. Just one each chooses low-confidence decisions, model disagreements or random sampling.

Among the 23 willing to operate with the ten-check limit, **20 favour checking the highest-cost mistakes**.

That reflects an intuitive concern with consequences. It also creates a useful opportunity to teach a more complete principle:


$$
\text{Expected harm}=\Pr(\text{AI is wrong})\times\text{Cost of being wrong}
$$

The costliest decision is not necessarily the best one to check if the AI is nearly certain to be correct. A less costly but highly error-prone decision may deserve review first. Conversely, disagreement between models or low model confidence can help estimate the likelihood of error, but says little about its consequence.

A sound review policy should combine both dimensions. The forced-choice survey did not allow participants to express that combination, so we cannot infer that respondents *ignore* error probability.
