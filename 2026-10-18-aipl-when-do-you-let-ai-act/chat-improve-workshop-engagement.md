**Reframed question:** How do I make a 2.5-hour workshop valuable for both active participants and passive viewers, and measure whether either group actually learned something?

The evidence doesn't yet say the workshop failed. It says the current interaction model needs testing.

### Here's what to do

- **1. Stop chasing participation.** 30 of 49 answered initially; 25 answered Q5. That's a substantial number. Stop the roll calls. First check whether Q5 was visible to people who already had the form open, and whether it was harder to answer.

- **2. Treat the next 90 minutes as a live experiment.** Invite 8-10 volunteers to actively participate. Everyone else can observe. Give them specific cases, ask what they'd let AI do, reveal the consequences, and discuss. Don't require everyone to respond.

- **3. Design for the recording too.** Repeat questions aloud, display the key answers, and summarize each exercise. Someone watching tomorrow should learn just as much from the discussion without needing to participate.

- **4. Measure learning, not just engagement.** Track three things:

* **Participation:** How many respond to a simple 30-second prompt? How quickly?
* **Learning:** Give the same AI-autonomy decision problem before and after. Has their judgment improved?
* **Application:** After a week, how many actually changed what they delegate to AI?

- **5. Don't blame Sunday morning yet.** Test a simpler interaction first. If even a one-click question struggles, the audience may prefer listening. If it works, the problem may have been Q5 rather than the workshop.

**My recommendation:** Run the remainder as a **recording-friendly session with a small live experimental group**. Then compare participation and learning. If engagement remains low, make future sessions shorter and recording-first, with a separate optional hands-on lab.

**The key question to answer afterward:** Did the quiet 80% learn less than the active 20%? We currently have no evidence either way. That's the measurement worth building.
