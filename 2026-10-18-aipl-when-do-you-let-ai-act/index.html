Act II · Trust

Trust is not one thing

At 41:13 Anand drew the autonomy ladder. At the bottom you ask AI for advice. Next it drafts and you rework the draft. Then it does the job and you check periodically. At the top it does the job and you don't check at all. The ladder is the backbone of the comic above. In the session it lasted about a minute, and the next dozen minutes moved it in a different direction.

To show that where AI is good varies by kind of work, he shared his treemap of OpenAI's GDPval results 42:36. Experts set realistic work tasks, models and human professionals both completed them, and other experts blind-graded the results. said live For accountants and auditors, the best model (Claude) won only 24% of comparisons. For personal financial advisors it won 64%. For sales managers, GPT-5 won about 79% 44:25. measured Those figures match the win rates in the data behind his chart.

Read GDPval narrowly

research GDPval (25 Sep 2025) covers 44 occupations, and each occupation has only a five-task “gold” subset. Experts compared finished deliverables, produced in one shot. It doesn't measure whether an AI can safely take an action, handle private data, or carry liability. A 64% win rate on report-style tasks is not a licence to let AI move a client's money.

He then told a personal story about pushing AI one rung higher. He gave ChatGPT his auditor's draft tax filing, access to his accounts, and a simple question: does this look right? It found a large discrepancy in his favour 46:15. The auditor pushed back with a technicality. He passed her email back to the AI, which came back with a legal argument. The auditor checked with her senior, who agreed, and the refund came through 48:42.

“When I push AI to act on my behalf one step further, I literally make money, or at least literally don't lose money.”

Anand 48:46

our reading Look at what “act” meant there. The AI filed nothing and sent nothing. It read, argued and drafted emails. A professional, and then her senior, checked every step before anything happened. Despite the way it was told, it is a case of the second rung done well, with expert reviewers at each step. The pushing paid off because someone qualified was there to check.

“I think it's a trust thing and the explainability which is holding us as humans to a certain extent… degree of trust and degree of confidence probably varies from person to person.”

Mukul 50:21

His own approach is cautious: test low-risk things, and learn from people who have already taken risks and tuned their setup. Ram then split trust in two and moved the discussion away from accuracy entirely.

“That trust is on your data, right? Then there is a trust on the actions.”

Ram 52:34

Ram talked about misuse: people asking AI for deepfakes, or for help breaking into accounts, because “AI doesn't really understand those, I would say, ethical boundaries of the outcomes” 53:35. He also talked about data. Every prompt goes to a company we can't see into, much as phone data quietly made us trackable. “Big tech companies are using this not only as a gold mine, but… as a leverage to control us.” 54:55 None of these worries goes away if a model scores better on a benchmark.

Trust 1 · Correctness

Is the answer right?

Error rates, hallucinations, benchmarks. This is the only one the rest of the session's demos measure.

Trust 2 · Authority

What can it set in motion?

Pay a claim, send an email, book a meeting. What are the limits, and can it be undone? This is Mukul's meeting invite and Ram's “trust on the actions”.

Trust 3 · Data

Where does what I give it go?

Confidentiality, retention, who profits. This was Ram's main worry, and several written answers echoed it.

Trust 4 · Reliance

Do people accept the right answers and reject the wrong ones?

Explainability and calibration. This is Mukul's “degree of confidence varies from person to person.”

our reading This split comes from this page, not the session, though each card is grounded in something said in it. Compare NIST's AI RMF Generative AI Profile, voluntary guidance that treats privacy, security, reliability and accountability as separate risks. On reliance, Cao, Liu & Huang (2024) found that showing calibrated uncertainty was not enough on its own to make people rely on AI appropriately.

Act IV · Earning autonomy

Five models, one answer

Anand's first answer to hallucination came from a support-desk problem. said live He described it as a client with lots of calls and chats who wanted AI to sort them into queues, cheaply and accurately 78:51. One example was “What do I need to do to register?” Some models filed it under Create account. The right queue was Registration problems. About half the models got it right.

The useful finding was not that models make mistakes. It was that they make different mistakes. In his correlation grid, most pairs of models barely agreed on which messages they got wrong 80:28. So he asked several of them and accepted an answer only when they all agreed.

Live, he gave the client's side of the deal 81:09. With five models agreeing, “I could go to the client and tell them I'll get you 99.3% accuracy. But that is only if all the models agree. 28% of the time the models won't agree.” 82:14 That means 72% of the work is done automatically, and the cost of five models is, by his rough estimate, about 2% of one person's cost.

Then came the line that reframes who should be doing this work:

“Managers are much better equipped to make AI more reliable than engineers because engineers are working with deterministic systems, managers are more experienced with indeterministic systems.”

Anand 83:39

Banks use maker and checker roles. Elections rely on many voters rather than one. “Humans are not reliable.” The processes we already use to manage unreliable people carry over to models. For a room of people who manage teams for a living, this was probably the most useful sentence in the session.

Four things to keep straight
  • 0.7% is the error among the 72% that were accepted, not across everything. The other 28% still need someone to decide them.
  • Reviewing 28% of the messages isn't the same as 28% of the work. The disagreements are probably the harder cases.
  • The published experiment uses a public dataset. The client story is how the speaker framed it. We haven't seen any client's data, savings or costs, including the “2% of a person”.
  • Agreement only helps if the errors are independent. Models trained on similar data, a shared bad prompt, or inputs unlike the test set can make five models wrong in exactly the same way.

Contracts that compile

The third technique gives prose the main advantage of code: an automatic signal for whether a result is correct. said live “Iterating as a human is time and effort. A compiler has the advantage that it can provide a correctness signal automatically” 88:45.

His first example was an InsurLE demo. InsurLE is a controlled language in which a motor-insurance policy becomes rules like a driver is fully eligible if they are age-eligible, license-compliant and have an acceptable driving history, down to age > 21 and months of experience > 12 90:07. An agent then checks a claim against each clause. For a sample claim filed in June 2024, the license was fine, the vehicle was authorised, and the blood alcohol was 0.14 against a limit of 0.08. The claim is rejected, and the record shows which clause failed 92:19.

A correction

In the session InsurLE was described as coming from Cambridge. research It comes from work associated with Imperial College London: Cummins, Dávila, Kowalski & Ovenden, an insurance extension of Robert Kowalski's Logical English. The demo itself is a separate open-source project.

The second example was Policy as Code 93:55. An agent reads a pile of regulatory guidance on financial promotions, breaks it into atomic, testable rules, and then checks marketing material against each one. (It was described live as European and US guidance. The demo's configuration actually uses the UK FCA's social-media and Consumer Duty guidance.) An SMS promotion failed. It highlighted the returns prominently and buried the “capital at risk” warning.

“Obviously the regulator will spot the same error. So don't.”

Anand, speaking for the agent 97:23

Notice how responsibility is split. When the agent finds a violation, you can reject the material. When it finds none, a person still reviews it 97:49. our reading That is a delegation policy in its own right. The AI gets to act only where a wrong decision is cheap: wrongly rejecting a draft costs a rewrite, while wrongly approving one could cost a fine.

What the rule engine can't check

our reading A rule engine can check the rules it was given. It can't tell you whether those rules mean what the original contract or guidance meant. Turning prose into rules introduces a semantic fidelity problem: a clause that was missed, an exception that was flattened, or a judgement call (“balanced view of risks”) forced into a yes/no test. The InsurLE paper names this as part of its motivation. Running the check is the easy part. Making sure the rules faithfully capture the text still needs someone qualified to spot-check them.

Which ten would you check?

Anand summed up his three techniques 97:54: agreement, benchmarking and checkable rules. “Once you have agents more reliable, then you will be able to let them act more autonomously.” 98:24 But the room had already answered a related question, at 9 a.m., before any of this was shown. It wasn't discussed in the session. It is perhaps the most revealing result in the data.

The demo and the room were answering different questions. Model disagreement points to the decisions most likely to be wrong. The room wanted to check the decisions that would be most expensive if wrong. Twenty-one of 33 chose cost. One chose disagreement. Nine said that checking 10% wasn't enough to let AI decide at all.

our reading Neither approach is enough on its own. A cheap decision the models disagree on wastes a reviewer's time. An expensive decision they all agree on can still be wrong in exactly the way that matters, and five-model agreement can't see that. A sensible review queue would rank decisions by roughly how likely an error is × how much it would cost, and then add the cases that need human sign-off whatever the probability, for privacy, legal rights or reversibility. The session didn't test this, and neither did the survey. It is simply where the room's instinct and the demo's maths meet.

    What to take away

    01 · Per task, not per person

    Delegation is a decision you make for each task. The same “impossible” task was routine for someone else in the room. Ask which tasks you would trust AI with, not whether you trust AI.

    02 · Can it act?

    Whether AI can act depends on access as much as intelligence. Calendar permissions, connectors and context decided what was possible as often as the model did.

    03 · Four kinds of trust

    Correctness, authority, data and reliance are separate problems. Mukul and Ram raised concerns that a better accuracy score won't settle.

    04 · Likelihood × cost

    Disagreement finds likely errors; the room wanted to check costly ones. Five agreeing models cut accepted errors to 0.7% on one benchmark, with 28% sent to people. Rank reviews by both.

    05 · Test the tip

    Benchmark the advice before you adopt it. A sensible prompting tip made answers worse in a small test. Turning policies into checkable rules helps, as long as the rules stay true to the text.

    06 · Measure the right thing

    Response counts aren't learning, and gut feel isn't a stopping rule. Write the decision down when you make it and check it later. The speaker hadn't done this yet either.
