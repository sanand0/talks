The exam has to pass its own exam.

A week earlier, the teachers of an AI-permitted course invented new assessments in public. By this Monday, three of those ideas were running — and the meeting became a test of the tests: what does a question really measure, what does checking an answer cost, and can the examiner trust its own grader?

Five hundred dollars to not set a question

Twenty-seven minutes into the meeting, between a list of client problems and a plan for 3D puzzles, Anand mentioned a bill. He said it the way people mention a parking fine.

“One question that we discussed last time that I simulated, unfortunately at a cost of $500. … Yeah, it just escaped me.”

Anand, instructor 27:24

The question was last week’s favourite. At the 12 October meeting the team had sketched it: scatter a client’s invoices across the class, so each student must trade with others to reconstruct their own. In the week since, Anand had an AI generate the invoices and then simulate how students would try to beat it — agents included, which is where I think it got out of hand. Witnessed The $500 is his own account, not an audited bill.

The simulations found the flaw. If many people and many agents may collaborate, and the puzzle has an optimal route, then one good route serves everyone: a hard exercise to frame given how we are organizing it. So he dropped it — or at least the way I had structured it. 27:56

What this meeting is about

$500 sounds like a lot to spend on a question nobody will answer. It is very little next to grading an entire cohort on a question that measures nothing. Inference The invoice puzzle failed the most useful test an exam can fail: the one it takes before students do.

That is the thread through this hour. On 5 October the course told students its philosophy: use agents, use friends, get real work done — and verify it. On 12 October the teachers invented questions out loud and a student asked them what they would actually measure. By 19 October some of those ideas were live, and the questions changed from “is this clever?” to “is this useful, what does it cost to check, and does it break?”

To see how, rewind to minute one — where three ideas from last week had already shipped.

… proposed GA3 · 17:56

When checking isn’t free

Then Anand turned to the next graded assignment, GA3, and to a problem with all of them. One of the things that I’m exploring is cost and time of verification. 18:00

The course’s remote online exam gives students very little time. The weekly assignments give a week or ten days, that allows agents more time and attention and plus as a group people can collaborate and solve, and therefore is not realistic enough practice. 18:24 So he proposed two changes. GA3’s deadline would stay, but questions would be released in stages, some with only a day or two to solve. And some questions would limit how often you can check an answer. He picked the example of a GeoGuessr-style location game a TA had built:

“We can now type in every single combination of latitude and longitude to the third decimal… and eventually figure out if it gets it right. So that’s a brute force thing. In real life, you would not have the luxury of such rapid iterations, nor would verification be free.”

Anand 19:48

The fix: It may be limited number of verifications or tradeoff verification against marks. So you decide to pay for one more verification if you think that that will substantially improve your score. 20:20 Try it. Three made-up students take one 10-mark question. Each answer they produce is right with some probability; each believes something about that probability. They check when they think a check is worth its price.

Hypothetical students & numbers
Checks allowed:
0

Model: an answer is right with probability p. A student checks if the expected gain — chance they’re wrong × chance a retry is right × 10 marks, using what they believe p is — beats the price. A failed check means a fresh attempt. Scores are expected values. Not TDS rules or data; the actual GA3 design was still being written.

With unlimited free checks, the guesser and the expert tie at full marks: the question measures stamina. Put a price on checking and the scores pull apart — and the gap that opens is not just skill but calibration, knowing how likely you are to be right. Inference That is no accident. Five minutes later, listing what Straive’s clients most want from LLM engineers, Anand put this first: systems that catch AI mistakes, so a sense of confidence calibration both from models but also from humans is going to be an important part. 26:12

But scarcity has a second edge. A one- or two-day release window rewards students who are free on those days; a check priced in marks taxes the anxious more than the reckless. Inference Fixed limits can teach real-world judgment, and they can also measure availability instead of ability. Someone in the room said so.

  • …and earned any marks at all
  • …by any route: alone, with agents, with friends, with a shared solver

What it can’t see

  • Whether answers leaked (a 100-shape bank, an endpoint)
  • How many checks were brute-forced
  • Who had the time, hardware or help
  • Whether anyone could do it again, alone, next month

Research The last line is the one that matters. In a randomised study of about 1,000 high-school maths students (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025), those given an unguarded GPT-4 tutor did 48% better on practice problems — and 17% worse on the later exam without it; a tutor designed to give hints rather than answers avoided the drop. Completion with help and learning are different quantities. A pass rate measures the first.

The exam has to pass its own exam. Its pass rate is where that test begins, not where it ends.

That is the humbling turn at the end of an ambitious hour. The team had shown a live market, a marketplace and a phone pen, and argued about verification budgets, agent harnesses and evals of evals. The most consequential instrument in the room was a percentage — and the most useful thing anyone did with it was ask what it counted. Inference None of this proves the course has found a new way to teach. What the meeting showed is a habit worth copying: put each question through students, agents and colleagues, find where it breaks, and say so on the record.

    Six tests every question should pass

    Not a proven recipe — an hour of a course team catching its own mistakes, written down so the next question can be caught sooner.

    01 · SPECIFY

    Name the ability, not the task

    “Agents can do anything. How do we figure out what is useful?” Write down what a student should be able to do afterwards — then let real users poke holes in the spec, as Preethi, JK and Carlton did. 03:24

    02 · SITUATE

    Put the hard part in the world

    A future number, a buyer’s choice, a hand on a phone. Real environments resist copying — and also exclude people without the phone, the time or the setup. Check who can’t reach it. 13:20

    03 · PRICE

    Decide what a check costs

    Unlimited free checks turn a question into a search. Priced checks reward calibration — and can tax availability. Say who pays, and cap the stakes while you learn. 19:48

    04 · ATTACK

    Break it before release

    Simulate students and agents attacking the question. $500 that buys a confident “drop it” is cheap next to grading a cohort on a puzzle with one shared answer. 27:24

    05 · AUDIT

    Check the answer surface

    Fixed banks, helpful endpoints and next term’s forum posts all leak. Ask what the grader reveals, how many answers exist, and what a shared write-up does to reuse. 54:40

    06 · TRANSFER

    Read the numerator

    A pass rate counts who scored above zero. Verify what was verified (1834 or 1653?), and test learning on a fresh case, alone, later. 59:08
