## The story worth telling

**Question:** What remains worth teaching when an AI agent can do the work, and when the grader can itself be gamed? In 12 October's meeting the team could propose tests for that. By 19 October, their proposals have already turned into a live prediction market, a human-services exchange and a phone-sensor game. The reality check is sharper: *Is this actually useful? What gets measured? What will a student learn? What does checking a result cost? And can the examiner trust its own validator?*

Find one cohesive, memorable narrative, not “nine innovative assignments.” The core should be concrete enough to understand without any knowledge of TDS or previous episodes. One promising story progression, NOT compulsory:

- **From ideas to incentives (00:43–17:48).** A predicted AI Pipe bill becomes a market; a *spoken promise* to help others becomes something classmates might buy; a mobile phone becomes a data-generating pen for a future ML task. Preethi's practical questions about price, offers, discovery, deliverability and marketplace ordering transform a vague “human skill” into a specification. JK adds legality; Carlton asks what ranking algorithm users will face. This is the previously abstract class *specifying real products in public*.
- **The cost of being certain (17:48–25:24).** GA3 experiments with short release windows and limited or score-priced verification. A GeoGuessr-style checker can be brute forced if a student has unlimited queries. But deadline, credit and checking costs also risk measuring availability/wealth rather than skill. Carlton raises eligibility/fairness, JK negotiates constraints on the record. A good visual can make students choose between one more test and accepting risk; label all numbers as illustrative unless sourced.
- **Ideas fail in the testing room (25:24–39:11).** Practical engineering needs verification/calibration and thoughtful search. The distributed-invoice puzzle from last week consumed **~$500 according to Anand's account** in simulations and was **dropped in its tested form**, not shipped. A fixed pool of 100 Tangram solutions, coupled with a filter endpoint exposing solutions, made another supposedly hard game shareable. The next proposals—3D cuts, harness optimization for unknown tasks, *evaluating the evals*—are intriguing but unproven. The educational achievement is **discovering a task is badly designed before grading a class with it**, not inventing clever puzzles.
- **The teacher meets the learner (39:11–59:33).** Preethi wants to learn safe agent setup; the team acknowledges a support gap. A mobile phone is proposed as a physical lab; niche bounties/ciphers promise real-world usefulness but raise verification/provenance doubts; a student's unexpected model-identification workaround teaches the faculty about a different solver path. At [58:17–59:25], the punchline: a “difficulty evaluation” is not an agent’s evaluation at all—it is just the historical percentage of submissions correct. This is a satisfying but humbling final turn: **performance statistics are not a measurement of individual learning or intrinsic task difficulty**.

An alternative stronger hook: begin with **the $500 abandoned exam** at [27:20], then reveal three exams that already exist and rewind to the design process. Another: lead with **the last minute**—the question “does the difficulty report use an agent?” and answer “No, deterministic”—then ask what numerical success *really* measures. Let evidence choose.

**The article must go beyond motivation to a transferable design principle:** specify the ability; create a real incentive/environment; adversarially test with students and agents; price the checks; audit leakage/fairness; test transfer, not merely completion. But make the conclusion an earned result of the *meeting*, not an unsupported claim that TDS has proved a new way to teach.
