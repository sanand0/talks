## The deeper question

Session 1: Should we let agents act? The survey said mostly "check everything"; in an intentionally limited review budget, most initially prioritized the **costliest possible mistakes**. Session 2 turns this into an implementation challenge: **a check itself costs money, human reviewers miss or disagree, confidence isn't always calibrated, the so-called correct labels are disputable, and an AI's promise not to take an action may not prevent it.**

A potential tension: **verification can be cheap when you recognize errors instinctively (your own home, your own teenager, your own code), prohibitively slow when you don't (1,400 migrated tests), useful when you calibrate it (BANKING77), and dangerously fragile when you only trust a sentence saying "don't reveal" or "draft only."**

The speaker takes a pragmatic, economic approach throughout. But there is a real counterargument: the value/rights involved in a decision, statutory requirements, human dignity, reputational harm and privacy **cannot always be reduced to the toy spreadsheet's rupee amounts**. Students—not the narrator—raise some of these limitations. Preserve the tension; don't retrofit a perfect framework he didn't state.

## Editorial hypotheses to test, not conclusions to assert

1. **The cheapest checker is domain knowledge.** True for Priya/Ketaki's recognition; false for work where the human can't know the correct label, or for privacy hazards they may overlook. An illustrated comparison of "can see it instantly / takes two days / requires calibrated benchmark" could help.
2. **The perfect confidence number is not the perfect answer.** T040 is both 100%-confident and agreed by the second AI, yet mismatched to the gold. Conversely, T083 is agreed correct by gold, while two practitioners disagree over its best operational routing. Are "true category" and "right next action" different things?
3. **What does a review budget buy?** The strongest outcome is not a winning group's names but an inspectable **cost of certainty**. Reconstruct one audience selection, a naive rule, and the errors they never saw. The experiment's financial penalties and perfect-review assumption are made up.
4. **The defense that works is sometimes removing capability.** As an instruction-protection demo, a secret word can be spoken despite "never say this"; but Swati's draft still didn't send automatically. Capability, approval and data access matter as much as wording.
5. **Checking the checker's label and cost.** The hard part may be *who gets to define success and consequence*. Bhavya's challenge pushes the story past "choose low confidence."
6. **The adult homework paradox.** Working professionals value AI for real work but may want children to do unassisted practice. This can reflect divergent goals, rather than inconsistency. A reader question ("What is homework measuring?") may be more insightful than a moral punchline.
