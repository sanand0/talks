Should a student even readskip? the exam?

Twenty-three minutes into an open meeting about next week’s assignments, the instructor of an AI-permitted course said that for a certain kind of question, the student should not even read it. The hour that followed is about what is left to teach — and how hard it is to grade — once an agent can read and solve the exam.

A recorded, unrehearsed working session of the Tools in Data Science (TDS) team at IIT Madras, held in the open so students can watch the assessments being designed and point out where they fail. Instructor Anand S with TAs Jaideep and Agrim; students Adwitiya and Kartikkumar spoke. Co-instructors JK and Carlton were away for Diwali week.

The open invitation

“Ideas for questions that we can include that will still teach if that question is literally copy-pasted.”

Anand asked every student to send such questions, copying all the TAs, and added that designing a question that still teaches when an agent can solve it is itself one of the skills the course wants them to learn. 25:04 On the call

That is the thread through this hour. On 5 October the course explained its philosophy: let agents execute; humans specify, orchestrate and verify; the exams are the curriculum. On 12 October the team designed agent-era assessments in public. On 19 October those designs met running code and the cost of checking. On 2 November the teachers asked whether there is anything worth teaching once the agent can read the exam — and found four problems on the way:

  1. The course changes while students are inside it, sometimes during the meeting.
  2. The teachers doubt their own job: if a lesson can be taught, an agent can teach it.
  3. Every “agent-proof” question has a leak: a meta-agent, a shared solver, or a WhatsApp group.
  4. A score can reward the wrong thing, and the students noticed before the teachers did.

There are two ways to read this moment, and both are fair. One: it is a lesson. Real forecasts are made inside systems whose operators can change the rules, and the skill is noticing, re-estimating and acting. Later in the hour Anand says exactly this, and tells students to build agents that watch the course for changes. 51:44 Two: it is a fairness problem. A rule changed on a Monday-evening recording reaches students only as fast as the announcement does. Students who check the forum hourly, or who have an agent watching for them, get the news first.

Our reading: both are true at once. A course that rewards monitoring a changing environment owes students a clear announcement of every change — otherwise it is grading who heard first.

IITeach yourselves out of a jobIf agents can explain the content, what is a teacher for?
Q2 09:00 – 14:56

“There is literally no point teaching what is teachable”

The course is changing so fast, Anand said, that the team now uses agents for much of the work of changing it — and those agents need briefing. So he had asked ChatGPT to draft a guidebook for the exam repository from the team’s transcripts, emails and course material: an agents.md in all but name, for any agent asked to write TDS questions. 09:00 He shared the draft and read out its first principle.

“If it is teachable, agents will learn it. So there is literally no point teaching what is teachable. It’s a weird way of putting it, but that’s where we are.”

Anand, reading from the draft guidebook 11:04

The next line turns the provocation into a design rule: what do we teach? What is it that humans need that agents won’t learn. 11:28 Agents are a tool on intelligence; give them tools, other agents and people, and drive them to get the job done — and verify it, because accountability stays with the human. The course’s older rule, “exams are the curriculum”, stays; the content pages are optional.

A caution in the margin: this is a deliberately provocative proposition, not a finding. Some things are teachable but unlearnable from text — motivation, values, the feel of a real deadline — and some things agents “won’t learn soon” may arrive next quarter. The useful question isn’t what will agents never do? but what must a person still decide, check and answer for?

Then he applied the rule to his own staff. He had dropped into a TA workshop the day before, where Mayank, a TA, was sharing how to create valid JSON. There must have been a reason for it. But I was thinking, hold on, why should we teach that? 12:56 Better, he said, to have an agent make an explainer, so that students learn to make their own explainers, and the explainer is published for any human or agent to reuse. Then came the challenge.

“How can we make ourselves redundant in the TA sessions? Meaning, what would it take for agents to be able to fulfill the need of the TA sessions in the first place?”

Anand, to the TAs 14:32

A teacher asking his TAs to automate themselves away sounds like an efficiency drive. The next six minutes showed it was something stranger, because the TA who answered had already done the opposite of preparing a lesson.

Q3 14:04 – 20:56

The TA who came unprepared on purpose

Jaideep had run one session, on WebMCP, a proposed standard for letting agents use web pages. It ended, he said, with him using Codex to build things in front of the students. 14:04 Then he explained how he had prepared.

“I have deliberately chosen myself to not look at WebMCP at all costs before the session. So each and every resource that is found is ad hoc.”

Jaideep, TA 15:04

The point was to start where his students were, with the concept equally new to everyone, and let them watch how he took it on with agents: neither “just tossing everything” at the agent nor “micromanaging things to the point where it’s better to do it by yourself” — how to find that balance. 15:32 Anand agreed at once: under preparation is an advantage in such sessions, because you’re transferring the broader skill, which is how do you deal with such a question. 15:56

This is a very old idea in new clothes. In 1991 Allan Collins, John Seely Brown and Ann Holum called it cognitive apprenticeship: in school, an expert’s thinking is invisible; apprentices learn by watching the expert work on problems the expert hasn’t solved yet, then being coached, then left alone. Research A polished tutorial hides the very part Jaideep was trying to show: the searching, the false starts, the decision to delegate or to look yourself.

His example of an unstated requirement is worth stealing. Suppose a question says only: a CIO wants a dashboard with this data. An agent can produce a dashboard. Q5 33:35 – 37:30

Every trap has a leak

Adwitiya came back with ideas for forcing students to read the question — prefacing them, honestly, with of course, it’s not a very ethical thing to do, but as long as teaching is involved. 33:44 The assignment page has a “Copy to clipboard” button for pasting questions into an AI. What if the copied text were altered on its way to the clipboard? Anand: Mayank had already done something like that, in the last ROE, he thought. 34:24

His second idea was subtler. A human reads “make sure your answer is correct” and checks twice. A frontier model reads it on a fiddly floating-point problem and may burn through a large share of its budget before declaring itself confident — and a student pasting the question would never suspect that line. 34:36

Anand’s answer is the most useful paragraph of the hour for anyone designing assessments. The idea is promising, he said, but not agent-proof: a capable agent will try a whole series of prompts and see which works. It could make a good ROE question — TDS’s timed, remote online exam — if each student got a genuinely different question, different in more than a number or a phrase. Otherwise, here is what happens:

“Have somebody create a solver for it and share with everyone. That solver will have done the full analysis, spent the first 30 minutes of the ROE getting to the result, and then tell people, ‘Here is the prompt … copy-paste this number and this text … from your question, put it in here. That is your answer.’”

Anand 36:24

He is happy for people to delegate, and happy that one person used an agent well. But a question that one solver answers for everyone teaches one person. His bar was explicit, and modest:

“Ten percent of people learning this is good, but it has to be 10%, not 1%.”

Anand 37:16

A question to put to every “gotcha”: does surviving it build a capability the student will use again — reading the instructions, budgeting effort — or only a suspicion of this particular examiner? A trap that the next model shrugs off, or that penalises students using screen readers or translation, fails the test even if it catches copy-pasters.

Q7 41:44 – 48:00

Run it. Stage it. Check the checker.

The last idea on Anand’s list came from his own history. He had catalogued about 10,000, 12,000 prompts he had given coding and non-coding agents, to extract the techniques he actually uses to specify requirements and verify output. 41:56 He posted the interactive list in the chat; that evening he published it as 52 Ways I Specify and Verify Agents (30 for specifying, 22 for verifying, ranked by his own judgment of frequency, impact and effectiveness — not a controlled test). Public, later that day He walked through the top three ways to verify.

1 · Run it for real

Give the agent an environment where its output can be executed and validated deterministically — a code runner, a chess engine, an API, a physical laboratory. 42:36 The frontier, he argued, is verification environments beyond code. Most students solved GA3’s 3D-face question with Meshy plus a chatbot, which is fine; few found an environment where an agent could build a 3D model, look at it and improve it. On face morphing he was blunt: most submissions are simply doing an animated transition between these, that’s rubbish; any in-between frame should look like a real photo of a real person. 44:20 Even finding that such environments exist is, he said, a useful skill to teach.

2 · Stage before the irreversible step

Could students be made to decide, across a series of actions, which are reversible — and spend tokens on checking where it can’t be undone? Anand floated it as an end-term question 45:12: out of these 10 things, which ones of these would you commit and deploy, or tell the agent to commit and deploy? 46:28 Try a small version.

Three checks, eight actions

A toy, not a TDS question

    Which actions are undoable is our labelling, for illustration. The design point is Anand’s: a budget makes checking a decision, and the decision should follow reversibility, not anxiety.

    3 · Verify the verifier

    In a number of cases, I’ve had situations where the test cases were wrong, where the judge prompt was wrong. 46:48 This is not a private complaint. In SpecGuardBench, a preprint from October 2026, four coding agents were asked to build validation harnesses from natural-language specifications across 291 tasks; none produced a faithful harness for more than 63% of them, often writing checks that mirrored the reference code rather than the spec.       Takeaways · a marking scheme for the examiner

      Six questions to ask before you set a question

      Not a proven method — an hour of a course team arguing with itself and its students, distilled so the next question can be argued with sooner.

      01 · NAME IT

      Name the human decision

      If you can’t say what the student must decide, check or answer for, the question is about the agent. Use the guidebook’s list — judgment, risk, diagnosis, context, accountability — as a starting set, not a law. 26:24

      02 · PASTE IT

      Paste it into an agent first

      And imagine one classmate’s shared solver. If the answer falls out unread, decide whether that’s fine. Aim, as Anand put it, for 10% learning, not 1%. 37:16

      03 · CHANGE IT

      Let the world move, out loud

      Changed caps, deadlines and inputs are realistic and teach monitoring. Announce every change clearly, or you’re grading who heard first. 08:12

      04 · PRICE IT

      Make checks scarce and stakes real

      Run it, stage it, check the checker. A limited budget turns checking into a judgment about what can’t be undone — if burning tokens is affordable for all. 52:24

      05 · AIM IT

      Grade the skill, not the route

      Hand-drawn or generated, coordinated or intuited: decide which you mean before students find out the score can’t tell. 56:08

      06 · VALUE IT

      Reward value, not activity

      An economy where every trade scores teaches trading. Someone has to judge whether anything useful happened. 54:36

      The comic ends with a slogan: build questions that still teach. The meeting was less tidy and more useful than that. It showed teachers willing to say, in front of the students being graded, that a question was solvable without reading, that a market rewarded the wrong thing, that a rubric didn’t care how you drew. And it showed students — Adwitiya with his traps, Kartikkumar with his smiley — doing exactly what the course says it wants: finding where the assessment fails.

      Should a student even read the exam? For some questions, honestly, no. The job is to keep writing the ones where reading, deciding and answering for the result are still the work.
