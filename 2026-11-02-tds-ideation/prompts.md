## The editorial contradiction

At [02:19], Jaideep says that making questions resistant to frontier agents is getting harder. At [23:34], Anand says something startling: **for a question that an agent can read and solve, perhaps the student should not even read it.** At [14:26], he challenges the TAs to **make themselves redundant**. But Jaideep explains why he deliberately came to a WebMCP/Codex lesson unprepared: so students could watch him actually figure out something new, decide when to search or delegate, and avoid both blind trust and micromanagement. Anand agrees. **The process, not the explanation of content, may be the lesson.**

That is a powerful central thread: **if the agent can get the answer, what does the student still need to learn to do?** The proposed answers include judgment, finding context unavailable to the model, deciding what work matters, recovering from mistakes, allocating a finite verification budget, predicting human behavior, coordinating people and accepting accountability. These are *the team's hypotheses*, not a permanent list of things agents cannot do. A stronger model may itself learn much of this. Show what humans remain **responsible** for, not an unsupported claim that agents will never gain the ability.

Two other unusually strong entry points:
- **07:57:** Anand raises the weekly IITM AI Pipe limit from $1 to $2, and asks TAs to alert students to revise their predictions of **AI Pipe usage**. A teacher has just changed the environment the class is forecasting. Don't claim independent proof of the effective account limit or prediction-market outcome without checking it.
- **53:42–54:25:** A student cannot see their Human Exchange offer on the marketplace. Anand explains there will be a second round—and admits that exchanging services only to earn marks is different from **creating services people actually find useful**. This is a real incentive-design lesson, not a triumphal account of the marketplace.

Choose **one strong story**, not a catalog of these openings. A promising 4-beat trajectory: (1) the course changes under students' feet; (2) teachers question their own purpose; (3) attempts to build agent-resistant exams run into the need for judgment, budgets and real-world verification; (4) the students reveal that success in the game's scoring system is not identical to learning or creating value. You may find a better arc; follow the actual evidence.
