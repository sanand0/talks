Rank evidence: (1) direct media recording + speaker; (2) dated reproducible local code/data; (3) contemporaneous original public posts/research; (4) inference/design proposal. ## 1. What does “teach what an agent can't do” mean?

**Direct transcript, approximately 09–12m and 23–31m:** Anand asks TAs to stop spending scarce live session time on facts that agents can explain, and to focus on specification, context, verification, judgment, accountability and making useful work happen. “If it is teachable, agents will learn it” is deliberately provocative. **It is not a proven universal property of agent learning**: physical-world experience, motivation, judgment under ambiguity, incentives, values, trustworthy source selection, and responsibility are not simply equivalent to a prompt. And even these might be further automated.

The question is not **“what is forever AI-proof?”** but **“what can a person still be required to understand, decide, verify or own?”** A student who instructs an agent to solve a static quiz might still learn nothing. Conversely, a student may learn how to investigate novel problems by watching someone operate that same agent.

**Primary education experiment:** Hamsa Bastani et al., *Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics*, PNAS 2025, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122 ; open text https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/ . In a large field experiment with high-school math students, AI help improved assisted practice, but unrestricted use was associated with **worse subsequent unassisted exam performance**; design safeguards narrowed/mitigated the gap. This supports the importance of independently demonstrating **learning**, not simply completion. It is **not** experimental evidence about IITM TDS, AI coding agents, an ROE, or the only appropriate form of checking transfer. A tool-positive assessment should test **transfer across changed constraints**, tool selection and justifiable accountability, rather than equate “no AI access” with competence.

**Classic teaching-the-process argument:** Allan Collins, John Seely Brown and Ann Holum, *Cognitive Apprenticeship: Making Thinking Visible*, American Educator, 1991, https://www.aft.org/ae/winter1991/collins_brown_holum . Modeling expert thinking aloud, observing strategies on unfamiliar tasks, coaching and gradually fading support are foundational ideas in cognitive apprenticeship. This is an especially good lens for **Jaideep's underprepared WebMCP demonstration**: process demonstration can be educational even when the artifact can be generated instantly. It **does not prove** that this specific session caused learning or that being underprepared is always best. Contrast a polished tutorial with genuine live discovery; neither automatically teaches transferable skill without learner participation/feedback.

**Important caveat:** Self-explanation and desirable practice can be automated as superficial artifacts. Students who merely submit agent-written “reflection” may satisfy a checker without learning. A valid design tests behavior or later decisions on a **new condition**, not the sophistication of a plausible-looking report.

## 2. Hard exams vs useful exams: watch the mechanism

The 2 Nov discussion at **20:56–24:59** and **33:35–37:33** repeatedly shows why a clever human-authored prompt is not a good “agent-proof” question: the student can copy the prompt into a stronger agent and let it optimize the instructions. If answers or an adaptable solver circulate, variations that merely change numbers do not measure independent problem solving.

Student-proposed tactics—clipboard edits, hidden side cues, complicated verification requests and midstream replacement of a previous number in a JSON-like resource—are **brainstorms**, not released policy. They could teach **prioritization, reading source data, handling updates and stopping automation**, but could equally teach no more than “suspect an obscure trap.” This is an assessment-validity tension, not an invitation to publish evasion techniques.

Potential anti-pattern to highlight: when a task asks students to make an agent stop after one test, a sufficiently capable meta-agent might read the assignment, write the stop instruction and run it without ever consulting the student. To test human allocation of limited checking effort, the task needs a **real choice under unknown consequences** and observed evidence of the individual's decision, not merely a prompt text.

**Emerging corroborating research:** Zhong et al., *SpecGuardBench: Can Coding Agents Construct Reliable Validation Harnesses from Natural Language Specifications?*, 12 Oct 2026 **preprint**, https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16870 . It studies 291 tasks from 13 real-world repos, where agents create validation checks and test cases. The abstract reports no evaluated coding agent produced a harness faithful to the natural-language spec on more than **63% of tasks**, and a recurring error of mirroring the reference implementation rather than permissible spec behavior. This is **task/agents/time-specific** and not proof that agents cannot generate evals or that TDS students will outperform them. The key connection: **verification criteria themselves may be wrong**. A plausible test suite is not sufficient evidence of meeting the intended requirement.

**Assessment standards:** National Council on Measurement in Education, *Classroom Assessment Standards*, https://ncme.org/involvement/committees/classroom-assessment/classroom-assessment-standards/ . Validity depends on the interpretation/use of the score and whether it represents the intended learning, with accessible, fair opportunity. A “normal score distribution,” a hard problem or a high proportion wrong is not evidence of fairness or learning. If a model can solve a question, that may be fine if students must demonstrate a distinct ability—but specify which.

**Reusable adversarial trial:** Try the full student-facing question with several capable agents and a human copying/forwarding a generic solver. Hold back changed/unseen tasks; audit answer leakage; independently validate scoring; examine access and cost; ask what student action was necessary. This is a proposed design protocol, not a reported TDS measured outcome.

**Market research:** Rasooly & Rozzi, *How manipulable are prediction markets?*, 2025 field experiment/preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03312 . Published experiments suggest some shocks/manipulation can affect market prices and persistence depends on liquidity and information. TDS's virtual-credit single-cohort market is **not** that field environment. More pertinent is the **causal distinction**: changing the environment being forecast can change forecasts; participants should update on new information and distinguish genuine forecast improvement from affecting the quantity predicted. Student trading itself need not cause model usage. Never describe a price as a statistically calibrated probability without checking the market mechanism.

**Fairness question:** Is “the syllabus changes, your agent should monitor it” a valid authentic skill? Possibly; real operations change. But official material changes need **clear announcement, transparent deadlines, predictable appeals, accessibility accommodations, and equal opportunity to observe changes**. Students who cannot run an always-on cloud agent should not silently be disadvantaged by an unannounced change.

## 4. Humans Only: the visual experiment is more nuanced than the meeting soundbite

**First-party experiment, prepared 31 Oct–2 Nov:** ~/code/tds-network-games/.private/trading/humans-only/{README.md,story.md,grading-insights.json,scripts/regrade.py,results.json,site/}. **The raw results include private class records: do NOT publish or hotlink them.** The exact public anonymized results page shared in **chat.md at 37:47** was verified HTTP 200 on 3 Nov: https://files.s-anand.net/pages/tds-2026-09-ga3-q5-humans-only/ . Prefer this to copying private raw data; still check individual visual reuse/attribution if extracting assets. The meeting's screen-share itself can be cited as a contemporary snapshot.

The locally analyzed game contains:
- **866 drawings** by **317 artists**, with **4,186 completed human guesses**.
- **Three model families:** GPT-6 Luna, GLM 5.3 Flash, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. Each tested each drawing **three times** with shuffled option orders; **7,794 model answers** total.
- In that analysis, human answers **3,345 / 4,186 = 79.9% correct**, GLM **2,077 / 2,598 = 79.9%**, DeepSeek **2,004 / 2,598 = 77.1%**, Luna **1,878 / 2,598 = 72.3%**. These are *aggregate trial accuracies*, not comparable independent participants; each drawing has 2–5 human guesses and up to 9 model responses. Different endpoints, settings and demographics prohibit universal capability conclusions.
- Particularly striking verified examples from story.md: **medal** recognized by **5/5 humans**, rejected by **all nine** model calls; **owl** correctly guessed by **all nine** model calls but **0/5 humans** (guessed five other birds). The reverse case prevents a simplistic “humans beat AI” claim.
- **11** drawings where humans were unanimous and none of the **three model families** was a majority-correct, based on the local experimental rubric and full processed set. The instructor's **“only eight”** near-perfect remark during the meeting refers to his provisional view/criterion. **Do not silently replace or merge 8 and 11.** Compare matching denominators, model trials and scoring version. Source grade-insights score formula treats each model family as one majority vote; older official score differs.
- The local analysis specifically flags **an image spelling the assigned word** despite an instruction not to use letters, choice-order effects, ambiguous ground truth (assigned target vs what the drawing could represent), and an AI ensemble that sometimes underperforms the best family. All are measurement caveats, not annoyances to remove from the story.

The most memorable result: **to fool AI while communicating to people, a drawing must do TWO things at once.** But the consequence of grading is tricky: highly legible to everyone is good communication but not “humans only”; unreadable to everyone isn't success either. Students could exploit the grading quirks rather than discover generalizable psychology. Agents and humans both produce diverse errors; a model's error on one drawing is not a permanent capability boundary.

**Sources for images:** ~/code/tds-network-games/.private/trading/humans-only/examples/medal.webp and owl.webp, gallery/, or the deliberately stripped public site. Never copy private datasets or student identifiers into talks. If using images, verify privacy/copyright, source dimensions, and published hosting; otherwise show frames from the already public course video.

## 8. What not to claim and how to verify

Do **not** claim: agents can learn literally anything teachable; every live demonstration improves transfer; agents can never make good judgments; a prompt trap is fair/agent-proof; getting a mark means authentic user value; model output errors prove deep conceptual inability; a spoken administrative change means everyone was notified; increasing the usage quota mathematically increases the market's final total; the “11 perfect” statistic contradicts “8 perfect” without aligning definitions; a video display guarantees publication of an edit; a blog post prepared *after* the talk was shown *during* it.

For numbers: label **unit, date, denominator, result version and source**. For graphics: distinguish hypothetical or anonymized from real submissions. For quotations: verify speaker and offset with WebVTT+video; do not reconstruct from noisy transcript prose. For causal assertions: say “may,” “proposed” or “in this experiment” rather than turning a local observation into a universal finding.

### Editorial “red team” questions

If the grader rewards credits traded, does it measure usefulness—or transactional coordination? If a student can copy a question to an agent, did any human learn a durable skill? If instructors change a spending policy midweek, are predictions genuinely updating to evidence, or reacting to unequal announcements? If some models miss a sketch but people recognize it, what happens when models improve or the multiple-choice menu changes? If the question says “check the checker,” how do we validate the **checker for the checker** without infinite regress? If a TA makes their prepared lecture unnecessary, which useful human capability is lost—and which is freed?

These are not answers. They are tests that make the story intellectually credible.
