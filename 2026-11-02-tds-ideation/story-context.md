## What this meeting adds

On 5 October the orientation taught “agents execute, students specify/orchestrate/verify; exams are the curriculum.” On 12 October the team invented unusual assessments that could still teach with agents permitted. On 19 October those ideas met live code, finite verification budgets, a discarded invoice simulation and real student feedback.

On **2 November**, the class confronts three layers of the problem:

1. **The environment won't sit still.** At 07:57, the instructor raises an AI spending limit while students are forecasting the aggregate spending. Noticing, interpreting and reacting to changed information is suddenly *part of the task*. Later he explicitly recommends monitoring the site with agents.
2. **The answer is increasingly worthless as a lesson.** “Why teach JSON when an agent can teach it?” leads to a radical challenge: make TAs redundant. But a TA who deliberately arrives without prior knowledge shows students *how* he discovers, delegates and checks. Perhaps the lesson is learning the process—not receiving content.
3. **Every attempt to test a durable skill can become a game with its own loopholes.** Student proposals to limit an agent's token spending are themselves solvable by a meta-agent; human-vs-machine sketches are not unambiguously meaningful; marketplace credits can be exchanged for marks without delivering useful services; obscure changes to prompts can become arbitrary traps.

The story is **a conversation between an instructor trying to automate teaching and students explaining where human learning actually happens**. Avoid treating either side as the winner. There is an open challenge: when the agent can do the task, **what change in student judgment, initiative, context-gathering, risk allocation or responsibility can a course validly assess?** That remains to be tested.

## Four-beat alternative structure

**1. The startling propositions (02:19–15:53).** “If it is teachable, agents will learn it”; “Why teach JSON?”; “TAs make yourselves redundant.” Jaideep's live lesson demonstrates the apparent contradiction. Build the central question: *what work should a student do that is not just work the agent can do?*

**2. Students redesign the teacher (16:16–37:33).** Adwitiya proposes extracting prompt guides, limiting agent token budgets and clever traps for blind copy-paste; Jaideep points to personalized context; Anand explains why a meta-agent defeats many proposed “agent-proof” GAs. Inspect Anand's agent-facing guidebook principles: tacit requirements, discovering context, diagnosing errors, calibrating risk, checking the checker, owning decisions. Faculty ask students to propose valid questions. The design task may be as educational as the exam, but there is no evidence it has been evaluated that way yet.

**3. Real experiments resist neat narratives (37:33–53:20).** Humans Only drawings contrast human and model judgments with reversals; authorship and rubric both matter. The concurrent specification/verification project (11,000+ agent prompts) makes the latent philosophy explicit and supplies inspectable techniques. GA3 face-model, morphing and rankings reveal that grading the *right* outcome needs a trustworthy evaluation environment; manipulation/coordination may become part of success. The instructor welcomes students monitoring changing course pages with their own agents—subject to honest boundaries.

**4. A student asks where the actual value is (53:20–61:40).** Kartikkumar cannot trade a recorded service because the marketplace hasn't shown it yet; Anand says second round is planned and admits mark-motivated swaps aren't the same as a useful service. Kartikkumar then questions hand-drawn vs AI-generated picture stories; Anand explains drawing craftsmanship was **not** the skill being taught. Jaideep's stroke-count suggestion may once again optimize an agent, not a student. Adwitiya ends with a proposed midstream change to a problem's source data to reward noticing and prioritization; Anand calls it promising, **not implemented**. The practical ending is: ask **what capability changed, what is the evidence, and what did the incentives reward?**

Either structure may be improved radically. Prefer the one where *people's actions drive discovery*, not one paragraph per syllabus topic.

## Evidence and claims hygiene

- The $1-to-$2 AI Pipe change is a **recorded claim/action**, not a verified account-level ledger, guaranteed market effect, final settlement or proven policy across all users.
- **Human Exchange** round 1 had listing/settlement timing constraints; round 2 is **Anand's announced plan**. Source implementation may lag behind this meeting. Never expose student audio and offers without consent.
- **Guidebook**: video shows intended publication; a 3 Nov source inspection found the course README contained a link but the target local file **was absent**. Do not claim successful deployment solely from the spoken instruction.
- **52 Ways** was written/published **after** the 2 Nov class; the underlying agent-prompt analysis was discussed in the session. Cite the post with timing noted.
- **Humans Only** sample and model panel are task-specific, version-specific. Model order/endpoint/reasoning and menu shuffles influence accuracy; human guesses are correlated and limited. Compare official vs experimental grading carefully. The story's medal and owl are strong concrete examples, *not universal facts about cognition*.
- **Face morphs**: critique is the instructor's qualitative evaluation, not a published quantitative benchmark; don't call models unable to morph faces.
- **Model and people capability claims**: “If it is teachable, agents will learn it” is a provocative extrapolation, not established. A missing rule or specialized tool can simply move the bottleneck.
- **Skills and scores**: high score on an AI-assisted task can coexist with little human skill acquisition; low score can reflect hardware/time/access. Do not equate leaderboard rank with learning.
- **Adversarial traps**: enough trickery to delay a generic agent may reward paranoia, punish accessibility or disappear with the next model. Does it teach a transferable decision procedure? If not, it is not a principled assessment.
- **Program-level source**: use public course and question instructions. Do not publish private question keys, examiner mechanics, signed URLs, unpublished grading scripts, student emails, private research notes or proprietary data.

## Ending to earn

A good reader should be able to ask: “I can hand this problem to a competent agent and get a good answer. So what exactly am I examining?”

Offer a compact assessment *experiment*: name the targeted **human decision**; hand the published question to a strong agent and to a shared solver; inspect where the answer came from; make it realistic, collaborative and accessible; verify with independent evidence; then assess a **new changed condition** that demands a fresh human decision. Don't imply any single technique proves lasting transfer. The enduring lesson may be **not to defend the existing exam, but to redesign its purpose every time agents change**.
