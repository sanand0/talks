The agent can learn that too

A student came to interview the teacher of IIT Madras’s notoriously hard Tools in Data Science course. Four minutes in, he was interviewing her about how Claude had written her questions. An hour in, they were taking his exam on a park bench. In between, every answer to “what should we teach humans?” met the same reply: you can teach an agent that.

Anand S, LLM Psychologist at Straive and TDS faculty, interviewed by Kusuma Komali Priya of the IITM BS Insider podcast team. A BS student joins for the live mini-exam. 67:40 recording.

Act I · Change the question A story told Research

NCERT wanted proof that tuitions don’t help

Kusuma asks what clients misunderstand about data. Anand goes silent for most of a minute. She checks in and offers to rephrase the question. 09:11 He was just choosing an example, and he picks a government one.

India’s school-curriculum body, NCERT, had run a large national survey of student performance. It came to Gramener with a request. Parents kept pushing for tuition, and NCERT wanted to tell them it doesn’t help. “We want evidence to show that tuitions don’t actually help. Can you see if that matters?” 10:05

The data mostly agreed: by and large, tuitions don’t help. But splitting by income changed the picture. As Anand remembers it, tuition “helps the rich a little bit. It hurts the poor” 10:32. Poor children who took tuition scored worse than poor children who didn’t. Then he did to Kusuma what he’d done all morning: he asked her to guess.

Act I · Change the question On camera

The question carries an assumption

The punchline isn’t about tuition. NCERT asked a narrow question: does this one intervention work? The data answered a bigger one: most of what drives marks comes from home, and “it just carries on generationally” 13:55. That changes what you think you can fix. Anand is careful here. He is “not saying that that is what they assumed,” only that “the question was framed in a way that is too narrow” 14:29.

“There’s two things that data does: One, it tells you the answers to questions you didn’t even know to ask. Second, it validates and invalidates what you ask. And the first, which is the blind spots, I think is where the bigger power of data is.”

Anand 15:06

Kusuma sums it up as two kinds of people: those who follow the data and those who misunderstand it. Anand corrects her gently, and the correction is the lesson: “No, I’m saying that sometimes when we ask a question, the question itself carries an assumption. We don’t always know what questions to ask.” 15:50

Then he gives his most quotable line of the morning. Asked how the field has changed since 2015, he points to a talk he gave last year, RIP, Data Scientists:

“Data science as we know it is dead.”

Anand 16:12

He adds one more sentence “for the audience”: agents can now do most of what data scientists did. What’s left is specification and verification. “How do I know how to ask good questions, and how do I know the agent isn’t bluffing its way through or making a total mess of it?” 16:23 The rest of the hour is those two halves, played out live.

Act II · The course A story told

He hates repeating himself, so he built an exam engine

How did a startup founder end up teaching? “Andrew reached out and said, ‘Here’s a course.’” 17:00 Anand had always liked the idea of teaching (“you’re like the god, your word is law”) until a few coaching-class stints showed him “I absolutely hate repeating myself. Act II · The course A story told

TDS used to be the easiest course

Every student has heard that TDS is one of the hardest courses in the program. Is the difficulty the point? The answer is a surprise: until 2023, maybe 2024, TDS ranked as extremely easy. The team had no plan to make it either easy or hard. The real design principle was this:

“You can’t find lazier people than us. Okay? Students may think themselves particularly lazy and adept at getting out of work. The TDS team is even better than that.”

Anand 20:00

Lazy meant every evaluation had to be automatic, which on the old portal meant multiple-choice questions. Those were easy when “everybody can sit, work together. Cheating’s allowed. Collaborations allowed.” The team worried the course wasn’t teaching enough. Once their own platform could check real work, the questions changed from “which library?” to “use it”:

“I don’t want you to sit and tell me whether I should use lxml or Beautiful Soup. I want you to write a program that uses lxml or Beautiful Soup and get the job done.”

Anand 21:10

The syllabus stayed roughly the same; the difficulty came from making students actually do the work. “And then with AI things got out of hand.” 21:46 That line gets a laugh, and it opens the central question.

Act III · What should we teach humans? On camera

“No matter what I say … it’s pointless”

Kusuma asks the question every TDS student asks: if AI writes the code, what is the course trying to teach? 21:55 Anand’s reply sounds like a dodge and turns out to be the most important idea in the interview.

“If I can explain the answer to that, I can explain the answer to an agent. … The agent is cheaper than the human. So, no matter what I say that the course is trying to teach, it’s pointless.”

Anand 22:06

Follow the logic. Say TDS teaches which libraries to use: write the list down and give it to an agent. Say it teaches the criteria for choosing a library: “list the criteria, give it to an agent as a skill” 23:03. Any lecture he could give, you could transcribe and hand to an agent. Which leaves a paradox:

“Only if I’m not able to explain it, is there going to be any teaching for a human.”

Anand 22:46

Then he hands the question back: “what do we teach in the agentic era? What’s your take?” What follows is a real exchange, and it is more interesting than a lecture because she doesn’t give up. Try it yourself.

Act III · What should we teach humans? On camera Designed exercise

What can’t an agent know? Class gossip.

The best of the surviving answers is also the funniest. Can a human forecast something better than an agent? Only if the human has information the agent can’t get.

“What information can an agent not have? Class gossip. ‘What assignment are you going to do? … How long are you going to be spending on this?’ is something that you may put on WhatsApp, or you may speak over a call … which is not available to every agent.”

Anand 25:51

This isn’t just talk. TDS has a forecasting question: how many AI tokens will the class use this week? A student who knows the class is about to start a token-hungry assignment has an edge over any model. The mini-exam Anand prepared for this podcast included a version of it: predict the weekly cost of AI Pipe, the course’s shared model gateway, from its public daily usage figures. It was zero-mark and settled after the window closed. Nobody tried it on camera.

The gossip was already in the interview

Remember how Kusuma prepared. Her Claude skill had the podcast’s format, and the guest’s LinkedIn and blog. What it didn’t have, and what she added, was what she’d heard about him offline: that he was “very kind of cool and very chill.” 05:52

That’s class gossip. It’s exactly what Anand says a human can still bring that an agent can’t. Asked what humans should learn, she struggled on camera (she was “not sure about the answer” 24:31). Act IV · Checking work smarter than you A story told

More consumers than the population of India

Kusuma has read his blog post from two days earlier arguing that specifying and verifying now matter more than doing the work. How does a beginner learn to spot when the AI is wrong? 28:17

“It’s hard,” Anand says, and tells two stories from consulting. In the first, he took an analysis to Alpesh, his manager at BCG, who glanced at it for a second or two:

“This number is rubbish.” “Why?” “Look, this is more than the total population of India. How can there be more consumers than the total population of India?” “Right, good point.”

Anand, retelling his BCG manager’s check 28:50

In the second, the blunder was his own. Advising a bank, he proposed dropping its demand-draft fees: customers would bring their drafts, and the bank would earn interest on the money until the drafts were collected. Then he did the scale check. Even if every demand draft in India went through that one bank, the interest would be less than the fees it gave up. “Completely stupid, right?” 29:06 Neither check needed the details. Each compared the result with one big number someone already knew.

Act IV · Checking work smarter than you On camera

We already know how to check smarter intelligences

That kind of shortcut, he argues, is not special to consulting. Every profession that has to trust work it can’t fully redo has invented one.

“Over centuries, we have, across different professions, found various ways of verifying smarter intelligences. They could be organizations, they could be societies, they could be people with more information than we have. … And the way we do this with agents is not very different. We just borrow from all of these professions.”

Anand 31:08

Act V · Getting the job done A story told On camera

“That is my problem, not your problem”

Kusuma asks the question people outside the course ask: doesn’t allowing AI sound like encouraging cheating? Where is the line, and what must students not do? 32:20

Anand answers from the employer’s side. New graduates who joined Gramener and Straive would work hard writing code to solve a problem. He’d point out an open-source library that already did it. Why write your own? Because using someone else’s work “feels like copying,” and if someone else did it, “What am I doing? I’m useless.” A week after he told them to ask colleagues for help, they still hadn’t. “Behind the scenes, there is the decades of training saying asking someone else a question is cheating.” 34:03

He doesn’t call that training wrong. A school wants to measure an individual, and collaboration makes that hard, so the teacher bans it. That meets the teacher’s goal. What it doesn’t do, he says, is make you “a productive person in a team setting.”

“Now that begs the question, then, how am I able to assess individual learning? That is my problem, not your problem.”

Anand 35:15

Kusuma doesn’t let that go. In an online degree, she says, one person finishes an assignment, it gets forwarded to the groups, and everyone submits it. How do you know anyone learned anything? 35:19 His answer is deliberately provocative. Suppose a student pays an institute ₹1,000 to do the homework.

“I would absolutely hire … as long as you can keep spending the 1,000 rupees getting the homework done, now I am getting your time and that person’s time because you’re spending out of your own pocket.”

Anand 36:20

Someone who can get ten people to do the work, he says, can be handed ten people’s work. “I am trying to teach how you get the job done.” 37:02 And yet even with collaboration allowed, some students scored zero on questions whose answer is the same for everyone. “If one student solved it, all you have to do is find one student who has solved it and get the answer. Why are you getting a zero on this?” 37:40 Knowing whom to ask is itself a skill some students haven’t learned.

Kusuma’s next question gets at the uncomfortable part: doesn’t it all depend on how each person approaches the problem? Anand thinks of it as a toolbox. If one tool solves all thirty problems for you, “good for you.” But he doesn’t know the right tool for now, and is sure it won’t be the right tool later. And “tool” is broad:

“How do I ask classmates? How do we learn to break up the tasks? How do I set up my coding agent? All of these are part of … who can I pay to take my exam for me could even be a tool.”

Anand 41:32

The tension doesn’t resolve. If one student can borrow an answer from another, that student’s score shows the job got done, not who learned to do it. Anand accepts the trade openly and calls measuring individual learning his problem. The re-test comparison is his partial answer. It isn’t a complete one, and he doesn’t claim it is.

Act V · Getting the job done On camera

“It’s not about fairness. It is the gatekeeper.”

Some TDS assignments are graded by an AI. Kusuma’s own teaser described one: draw an object in under a minute, then show it to friends and to three AI models. You score when the humans recognise it and lose marks when the AIs do. 00:21 So how do you know an AI grader is fair, with no human involved? 41:51

“The AI grader is what we’re trying to convince. … Just like I have to understand human psychology, I have to understand LLM psychology and crack it.”

Anand 42:04

His comparison is a CV screen. Recruiters already use AI to read CVs, and you can’t make it fair, only convince it. A human screener isn’t guaranteed to be fair either. At least an AI can be tested any number of times. That reframes the assignment: the task is to persuade a machine gatekeeper, which is a real skill. It doesn’t fully answer Kusuma’s question, though. Persuading a grader you face once isn’t the same as an institution grading every student consistently, and the recording leaves that open.

She pushes for a concrete case: a disagreement where the AI did a bad job. Anand goes to get his laptop, sets an agent to search for examples, and comes back a minute or two later. “Okay, it’s still working and it’ll probably find me some good examples. I will come back.” 45:19 It didn’t come back before the conversation moved on. Even the guest’s delegation needs time the podcast didn’t have.

So what should we teach humans?

Anand never gives a list, and his argument explains why: anything he can write down, he can give to an agent. But the hour on the bench shows the answer anyway, because every useful human move in it happened at an edge the agent couldn’t reach.

Kusuma added what she’d heard offline to the script Claude wrote. Anand asked the question back instead of answering the one on the card. NCERT got a better question than the one it asked. A manager caught a wrong number by comparing it with India’s population. Anand’s agent failed because he hadn’t given it a working key. A clerk knew to rename the file abc.pdf. The student recognised Japanese from anime, then found that recognising it didn’t matter. He also built a chatbot nobody asked him to.

None of these is a permanent human advantage. Next term, some will belong to agents. That is the real curriculum: work at the edge of what agents can do, and move with it. Assessment is still unsolved. When a friend, an agent or a paid service can get the job done, the course can see that the job got done but not always who learned. Anand calls that his problem, not the student’s, and the honest status is: still being worked on.
