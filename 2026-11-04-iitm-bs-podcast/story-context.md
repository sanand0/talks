# Story context — IITM BS Insider podcast, 4 Nov 2026

## The story worth telling

Working headline: **What Should We Teach Humans?** Alternative: **An Exam Your AI Is Allowed to Take**.

A student interviews a professor about his unusually difficult course. Within four minutes the professor interviews the student about how she used AI to write the questions. By the second recording they are actually trying his exam. The interview itself demonstrates the argument: if the machine can do the visible work, the human's job is to decide what work is worth doing, discover what the machine lacks, and check whether the result works. But those jobs, too, can increasingly be delegated. A useful course has to keep moving toward the boundary.

Don't turn the piece into an advertisement for TDS or an exhaustive career interview. Use the human conversation, the genuine disagreements and the live experiment to make the reader experience the course. The host is an active practitioner and interlocutor, not a foil. The unresolved question of fair assessment matters. The narrative must distinguish competent delegation, individual skill, and evidence of learning; a passing result alone cannot identify which mechanism produced it.

## Optional editorial map

Time references below are approximate positions in the combined recording; the Q&A begins at 48:14. Check transcript and audio for exact wording.

1. **The interviewer becomes the case study.** 03:30–06:30. Host used LinkedIn and blog material, a reusable Claude podcast skill written as Markdown, and individual context. 2. **Data science is not about answering the question you were handed.** 09:19–16:45. NCERT's tuition question leads to segmentation, parent education/occupation, TV and play, and unexpected associations. Anand's account is supported by his published chapter/blog; describe correlations, not causal effects or speculative regional family explanations as proven. The pivot is from known questions to blind spots. The provocative “data science as we know it, is dead” is qualified immediately by specification and verification.
3. **Why the easiest course became hard.** 17:00–22:00. Automated assessment initially meant simple MCQs; a programmable exam platform enabled asking students to accomplish actual tasks, including those using LLM APIs. Difficulty was a side effect of authentic tasks, not the objective. The course team jokes about being lazier than students. The course's open collaboration/AI policy applies to this course, not automatically to other courses or institutions.
4. **Teach a human something an agent can't already do?** 22:00–28:20. A recursive challenge: teaching an agent to do a task, then teaching an agent to talk to an agent, leaves the human question open. Examples: human buyers/preferences in a marketplace, class gossip not accessible to every agent for forecasting, live storytelling, tools at the current frontier. These are contingent advantages rather than claims of permanent human superiority. Let the reader pause on the host's uncertainty before revealing examples.
5. **How to check work smarter than you.** 28:20–32:20. BCG manager catches an implausible estimate by comparison to India's population; Anand's bank draft fee recommendation fails a scale check. Borrow practices from auditing, courts, science and other professions. Ask for sources and verbatim passages; cross-check with independent models. A cited quote being present is necessary but not sufficient for claim truth. Connect to the 2 Nov article on 52 specification and verification techniques.
6. **Is collaboration cheating, and how do you know learning happened?** 32:20–43:00. Reusing existing software and asking colleagues is valuable at work, whereas independent assessments have a different measurement goal. TDS treats finding help, getting things done and learning new tools as part of the curriculum. Anand reports repeat-question transitions (20% wrong-to-right versus 5% right-to-wrong for an Ollama-related skill; 1% improved versus 9% worsened on configuration precedence). Present as recollections, not verified experimental results; different task variants and denominators matter. Do not use these as causal evidence. Host's question about AI grader fairness is substantial: distinguish the exercise of optimizing for a machine gatekeeper from institutional obligation to grade consistently and fairly. The transcript does not provide a definitive audited answer. A promised search for grader examples is interrupted; don't fabricate the result.
7. **An AI failed the exam because its human did.** 45:37–46:50. At Paradox, Anand reports that a student's agent got 10/10 while his got 9/10; he had omitted an AI Pipe token. Model/access differences complicate the causal comparison. Treat as his account of a practical lesson: give an agent the permissions, context and resources needed for the job. Link the earlier talk and exam story. This is not the 4 Nov podcast's exam score.
8. **Now try it.** 48:20–1:00:54. Student hands a problem to Codex and asks about the Ask AI control; he discovers a model dropdown. Discussion about modifying a difficult interface rather than waiting on someone else; the passport upload anecdote illustrates the missing mundane detail a human official supplied. 9. **Understanding is sometimes optional; accountability isn't.** 1:00:52–1:07:40. Discuss when an agent can understand and handle routine customer questions, when a human should take over, the cost of escalation, and a participant's prototype chatbot that never got deployed. Personal software avoids many organizational hurdles; end with the insight that human choice, evidence, and consequences still matter. Avoid private internal case details unless cleared. - Another strong visual: workflow diagram question → agent → evidence → human check → revised question, with the human/agent boundary moving over time. Use examples actually discussed. Make the questions and failures the center of the visual language.
- For alternative public perspective, see student feedback links in links.md. A short positive and critical pair can be useful if clearly labelled as independently sourced context, not quotations spoken in the recording. No email/phone/roll numbers.
- Closing takeaways for students: define success before delegating; provide tools/context; investigate unexpected failures; benchmark on your own tasks; collaborate when allowed; verify independently; learn how to learn at the shifting frontier. For educators: design authentic tasks, assess transfer with appropriate controls, separate process and outcome when necessary, and audit the assessment itself.
