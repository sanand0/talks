## The surprising central story

The course says an agent should be able to catch a sudden deadline, collaborate with a roomful of other agents and humans, discover a specialized tool, act in 20 minutes, and check its answer. A student builds a deadline tracker. Anand opens it live—and discovers that its giant, simple countdown is *wrong* for the most important upcoming questions. JK laughs: **“We will need our own tracker for all these trackers. Meta tracker.”** [~12:28]

This is a perfect microcosm of the entire meeting: every solution creates a new problem of specification and verification. The question isn't whether an agent can watch the site; it is **who checks that the watcher correctly understood the changing rules?** And if the course intentionally tests that skill, **how does it keep the test fair to students with jobs, families, accessibility needs, time zones and unreliable connectivity?**

This episode follows the earlier ideation talks:
- 12 Oct: “The Exam Is a Hypothesis”—what exams could teach if agents solve everything?
- 19 Oct: “The Exam Has to Pass Its Own Exam”—how design proposals survive actual code, budgets and feedback.
- 2 Nov: “Should a Student Even Read the Exam?”—the difference between obtaining answers and learning judgment.
- **9 Nov: the team openly defends why the exam changes, demonstrates live multiplayer assignments, exposes unintended failure modes, and admits that measurable learning remains unfinished research.** The faculty themselves are learning by observing students.

**2. A business simulation with a roomful of rivals (~22:30–33:30).** A genuine, code-backed test of fast learning: Food Court. **4. ## The crucial counterarguments

**A stopwatch isn't proof of learning.** A student may be excellent at negotiating but unavailable at any of three live times. Another may use a remote agent to trade discounts; high grades don't automatically show human learning. The game could reward access to high-performance tools, English communication, social networks, willingness to collude or prior domain experience. Fairness requires enough accessible alternatives, clear announced rules and predictable support.

**A failed login is not a character-building exercise until software has been checked.** Even if many users succeed, a specific identity, session or auth bug can affect a minority. The 9 Nov meeting explicitly included multiple reports. Review the TAs' validation, browser differences and current fixes before portraying a student as insufficiently resourceful. Anand's mention of deliberate Safari failures is an instructor's statement; independently verify whether and where this was actually engineered and assess equity.

**A wrong counter can be a teaching opportunity—and a teacher's responsibility.** A simple tracker accurately reports the terminal deadline yet misses urgent *sub-deadlines*. A user-facing countdown needs the correct event ontology: deadlines, windows, repeated slots, qualifications, scoring, accessibility. This is really a *specification* error more than an arithmetic one.

**Better marks on a later task do not establish learning transfer.** The Nov 4 local ChatGPT study compares paired results from the same students but flags different task difficulty, changing agent capabilities, selection, ceiling effects and confounding general proficiency. Example: GA0 Ollama → GA2 tunneled LLM API, 687 matched students: 146 improved (21.3%), 37 worsened (5.4%), 504 unchanged. But unrelated earlier tasks predicted later Ollama success almost as well. Termlog retention showed large persistence gaps. Cite the report as exploratory; publish only independently corroborated aggregates, never private student rows. Do not claim randomized causality.

**Learning is not automatic, even when someone completes a task.** A 2026 Education Endowment Foundation synthesis for 16–19 learners explicitly recommends teaching metacognition through planning, monitoring and evaluation with modeling and guided practice, and warns not to assume students develop these unaided. JK's closing call for deliberate retrospective reflection is more evidence-aligned than an unrestricted “just figure it out”. The TDS population and assignment types differ from the reviewed cohorts.

**Collusion can be both collaborative and distorting.** The Food Court's mixed 60/40 grade rewards some collective efficiency plus individual success, but doesn't force aligned incentives. Partner coordination is part of the game, not evidence every participant learned game theory. In Time Use Charts, unanimity before submission creates a deliberate strategic deadlock and a potentially unfair dependence on group attendance. The game’s mechanics are facts; the learning effect is a hypothesis.

## High-leverage visual/interactive suggestions (select 2–3 only)

1. **Which deadline?** Interactive week view with GA4 EOD on 15 Nov and six *attendance windows*, highlighting why a one-number countdown misleads. The two games have different session dates and lengths. Accurate, legible, with IST and optional browser-local time, no invented schedule or access claim.
2. **20 minutes in the food court.** An explorable illustrative discount grid (4 stalls × 12 months) where choosing 0/10/20 changes outcomes; ideally compute from the actual public model or show a simplified labeled toy, and let readers test collaboration vs price wars. Don't pretend a toy simulation reproduces the live engine.
3. **The learning matrix.** A 2×2 before/after paired pass/fail quadrant for 687 actual Ollama-related student comparisons (with citation and caveat), then flip to an unrelated-task control revealing why improvement isn't necessarily subject-specific transfer. This is a powerful “wait, really?” graphic; only if the aggregate reproducibility is independently checked before publication.
4. **Three different “learning objectives” for one image.** Preethi: observation; Hritik: model perception; JK: depth/geometry; Anand: tool discovery/verification. The ambiguity may be productive; the rubric must know what it actually measures.
5. **Tool beats toil.** Compare a simple crossfade between two faces with a proper geometric morph whose middle frame remains plausible. Use vetted public demo assets, avoid identifiable student submission faces; avoid reproducing protected music/video content.
6. **Two minute postmortem.** Ask the reader to specify: What did I try? Why? What would I change if time, team or input changed? How would I know the answer is wrong? This is a more actionable ending than “AI changes education”.

## Editorial ending

Don't conclude “this course has proved learning,” because it has not. Don't conclude “surprise exams are unfair by definition,” because some are authentic rehearsals of uncertain, time-sensitive work. The final tension is **designed discomfort versus accidental exclusion**.

One memorable closing thought: a student created a deadline watcher. The instructors realized it needed a checker. The faculty are building assessments to detect student learning; their own measures need to be checked, too. In a course teaching verification, **the verifier itself is the most important exam**.
