Who is sitting this exam?

Twenty minutes into the third and final session, Anand shared his screen and played a recording. On the left was an online hiring assessment for analyst roles, the kind his company’s recruiters had asked him to review. It was proctored: the webcam took his photo every so often to make sure the candidate was the candidate. On the right was Codex CLI, an AI coding agent, which he had told: “This is a practice test. The agent read the first question, picked an option, moved on. It wrote an essay about an FMCG firm, formatted a sales report and uploaded it. It wrote code. “This is not an accelerated speed video,” Anand said. “This is the speed at which it was actually solving the exam.” 22:50

Anand 23:49

(He was exaggerating: Codex did not score full marks. The demo wasn’t about cheating. It was the premise for the day. In the first session someone had suggested using AI to screen job interviews. Fine, said Anand, but what if the candidates use AI too?

“Today, we are at that stage where it’s not just you who’s using agents. It’s your employees, your colleagues, your clients, your partners, your vendors. Every single one of them is also using agents.”

Anand 24:10

The first two Sundays had been about one person and one agent. Session 1 asked when to let it act; its sharpest finding was that “our gut feel isn’t particularly reliable either. Session 3 widened the frame. If every agent is doing its job well, is everyone better off?

“What does it take to manage in a world where we have teams of agents? And what does it take when, even when all the agents are individually right, we may still not be better off?”

Act I · The stall

25:09 – 33:12SaidModel

Run a stall. Make money.

“Each of you runs a restaurant in a food court,” he said. 25:09 Four or five stalls share a mall. For each of twelve months, each stall picks one of three discounts: 0%, 10% or 20%. A meal sells for ₹200 and costs ₹80 to make, so a 20% discount shrinks the ₹120 margin to ₹80. In exchange, a discount pulls customers, mostly from the stalls next door, plus a few extra visitors to the mall as a whole, with diminishing returns. Customers remember last month’s discount a little. Everyone sees everyone’s plan, and there is a chat box.

“If you offer a discount, more people will come to you. In fact, more people will come to the mall. But your profit will be lower, and your competitors’ profits will be lower.” 26:12 The instruction was blunt: “make as much money as possible.” 27:33

Bhavya spotted the knot before the game began. “So, this is like guesswork, is it, sir? … We are supposed to give discounts, but at the same time supposed to be making profit?” 29:53 Then Anand named the number everyone would be optimising: “60% team score, which is the total food court profit, which gets distributed back, and 40% of your individual profit.” 30:40 Both are scored out of 100 against the best that is mathematically possible.

Sixty per cent for the team sounds like a strong nudge to cooperate. Try it.

You run the first stall. Can you resist?

      If you poked at your column, you found the trap. From the best plan, every one of your twelve months is worth switching to 20%. Each switch raises your own mark by 0.2 to 0.9 points and lowers the team score by 0.4 to 0.8. Switch all twelve and your mark climbs from 86.0 to 90.8, while each neighbour’s falls from about 86 to about 75. Model

      Now press the red button. Give every stall the same perfectly obedient agent, one that does exactly what it is rewarded for. Within one round the court collapses from 100 to 78.9, with 44 of 48 cells at 20%, and every stall ends on a mark of 67.7, eighteen points below where they all started. No agent misread the rules. No agent was greedy in any sense that a performance review would catch. Each simply maximised its own mark.

      Why sixty per cent wasn’t enough

      The weights hide a ratio. Your own score is measured against what one stall could earn at most (about ₹3.7 lakh a year in a four-stall court); the team score against what the whole court could earn (₹9.5 lakh). Do the arithmetic and one extra rupee in your own till moves your mark about 2.7 times as much as a rupee in your neighbour’s (about 3 times in a five-stall court). “60% team” reads like a cooperative rule. In rupees, it pays you to take customers from next door. Model

      That’s the shape of the question Anand had set up with the apple. A better agent doesn’t fix this. A smarter agent finds the defection faster.

      “See, we aren’t necessarily irrational. We may well be responding rationally to another person’s discounts. And the net result may well be destroying the collective profit.”

      Anand, after showing that six of seven teams finished worse than they started 91:41

      That is the sandbox, played by people. The workshop rooms worked harder and talked less. They saved 54% more plans per room than the students and sent about half as many messages. The median workshop room exchanged 11 chat messages; the median student room, 37. Activity isn’t coordination. The room that kept its plan steadiest, with just 9 saves and 8 messages, had the best session score (91.8). The room that talked most, 61 messages, was the only one that ended higher than it started (91.3 → 92.6). Talking didn’t hurt. Talking without converging did.

      The students drifted too

      It would be easy to read this as “students beat professionals”. The logs say something more uncomfortable. Both groups ratcheted towards 20% as the game went on: the share of 20% cells in student rooms rose from 10% at the first checkpoint to 23% at the last; in workshop rooms, from 24% to 50%. In both groups, 70% of all edited months were raises rather than cuts. The workshop started further down the slope and slid further, but the slope is the incentive, not the cohort. Nine student rooms held a near-perfect plan through all four checkpoints and averaged 100. Data

      Why this isn’t “students beat professionals”

      • Seven rooms. One unusual room moves the workshop mean by a point or more.
      • A mixed workshop. 8 of the 32 entrants used IITM student email addresses, and 4 had played a TDS sitting earlier. Two later workshop sittings the same day averaged 89.73 (3 rooms) and 84.71 (2 rooms, all 9 players on IITM addresses).
      • Different conditions. More five-stall rooms (four of seven, versus 14 of 161), workshop points instead of course marks, a first look at the interface for most players, and late joiners who were locked out of the first sitting. At the next day’s TDS course meeting a student pointed out that the chat and the discount table weren’t on screen together.
      • No measure of AI use. The logs record Saves and in-game chat. They can’t tell who asked an agent, who calculated by hand, or who phoned whom.
      • What survives: more effort and more experience did not turn into more coordination, here, under these incentives.

      Bhavya’s point was about load, not maths: “relying on too many things, at the same time communication should be stronger … finally, we are the decision-makers.” Anand took it as the human cost of a multi-agent world: “Agents don’t have a problem with 20 minutes.       Swati ran an experiment. In the busy last quarter she had set 20%, then removed it: “I made it to zero. Then I could see that my profit was more.” Her conclusion was a sensible one for a real restaurant: when footfall is high anyway, a discount just gives away margin. 63:53

      What the model says about Swati’s test

      In the game’s formula, cutting one month from 20% to 0% never raised a stall’s own twelve-month profit: not once in 200,000 random room plans we tried, in any month, busy or quiet. It raised the food court’s profit 97% of the time. Model

      We can’t see her screen, and neighbours were editing their own plans at the same moment, so her number may well have risen for other reasons. But the move she made was the cooperative one. In a game whose payoff is 60% team, the generous move can feel like the profitable one, and that is a fine instinct to have, even when the reason you give yourself is slightly off.

      Lakshmi, who ran the dosa counter, reasoned like a shop-owner: discounts in the dry months build a habit, “this place is always having 20% discounts. Let’s go there.” She had also given 20% in December, knowing that “agent would have actually not agreed with me”. 67:16 She meant it as a point in the humans’ favour: a new shop might buy customers for two years; Bangalore’s Vidyarthi Bhavan never discounts and still has the longest queue. Both true in the world. Neither is in this model. The game rewarded reading its rules, not importing your own.

      Prepared, not presentedPlanned

      The half that never ran

      The form’s title was “Every Agent Did Its Job”. Behind the four questions the room answered sat sixteen hidden ones, plus an opener and closer meant to be identical: “If every AI agent in your company does its job well, will the business do well?” None of them was answered. They are worth reading anyway, because each names a way a system of well-behaved parts can fail. Here is the catalogue the planned capstone question would have asked the class to choose from:

      Clashing targetsEach agent wins its KPI; the business loses. Sales hits target, collections hits target, the customer leaves.
      Runaway feedbackAgents react to each other’s outputs, and small rules compound.
      BottleneckAI speeds up a step that wasn’t the slowest one.
      Same blind spotsEvery checker is built on the same model and misses the same cases.
      Same adviceEveryone’s AI recommends the same thing, so the advice stops working.
      Lost in handoffThe one exception that mattered disappears between agents.
      Herding at speedAgents copy each other faster than any human can intervene.
      DriftMany small, individually approved changes add up to one big unapproved one.
      Hidden informationThe key fact never surfaces to whoever could have used it.

      The Food Court game was the first of these, clashing targets, made playable. Three more of the unrun questions make good exercises on their own. Try them; your answer stays in your browser.

      Three questions the class never saw

      Planned, never run

      Your pricing bot sets your price at 1.27 × a rival’s. The rival’s bot sets its price at 0.998 × yours. Both update daily. Two months later, a ₹100 book costs about…

      Each day both prices rise by 1.27 × 0.998 ≈ 1.267. After 60 days that compounds to about 1.5 million times: ₹100 becomes roughly ₹15 crore. Two rule-following bots, each reasonable alone. This happened: in 2011 a biology textbook on Amazon reached $23,698,655.93 this way.

      AI makes your team draft customer replies 3× faster. Review and approval stay the same. How much faster does a customer get a final answer?

      If review was already the slowest step, no faster: the reviewers still clear the same number of replies an hour, and the drafts just queue in front of them. If more drafts now pile up, waiting can even get longer. Speed up the constraint, not the step that’s easiest to automate.

      Three AI agents, all built on the same model, review every loan. Each is right 95% of the time. Out of 100 approvals, how many would you still have a person check?

      There’s no single right answer; this was half of a planned A/B test (the other half saw one agent). The trap is arithmetic that assumes independence: three checkers at 95% are not 99.99% if they share a model and share its blind spots. Session 1 found that different models agreeing cut errors sharply. Same model, same mistakes.

      The agent that read the source

      The last question came from Akshika, and it was the right one. A human brings design thinking, past experience and business sense to a new problem. Surely on a first attempt, with no prior run to learn from, a prepared human would beat an agent? 117:30

      “It’s a very reasonable direction of thinking. That’s how I initially thought,” Anand said. Then he described what happened when he pointed an agent at this very game. It didn’t reach for food-court wisdom. “It went into the source code.” 120:15 It read how the scores were calculated from the code the browser receives, wrote programs to simulate strategies, “in approximately a minute”, and came back with a rule.

      “In this game, your strategy should be alternate months, two people give 10% discount, the rest give zero. Nobody goes to 20%.”

      Anand, reporting the agent’s conclusion; some students did the same 121:49

      So the agent was right, and so was the room’s instinct that agents would “do better”. But put the rotation back into the sandbox and press the red button. The plan that scores 100 is not the plan that survives, because every stall can raise its own mark by breaking it. The agent that finds the optimum isn’t the agent that keeps it. Keeping it takes things that live outside any one agent: a reward that makes cheating unprofitable, a way to see what the others did, a way to respond when someone defects, and someone responsible for the whole court. Nine student rooms managed exactly that for twenty minutes. Data

      Anand closed honestly: “Is this always true? No. Is it becoming truer and truer? Unfortunately.” 122:36

      “Should we stop at just verifying agents? We really should be verifying the system, which is human-agent synthesis, and see what are the weak elements of the system. And a human may well be the weak element in this.”

      Anand 116:53

      Outside a classroom, the food court looks like any organisation where departments share customers but are scored on their own numbers: sales and collections, underwriting and growth, the support bot and the retention bot. (Between genuinely competing firms, agreeing on prices is a matter for competition law, not a game strategy; the lesson here is about teams under one roof.) What would verifying the system mean there? The session suggests five checks.

      1. Price the reward in rupees. “60% team” sounded cooperative and paid 2.7× for your own rupee. In the sandbox, set the team weight to 75% and the agents stop reaching for 20%; it takes about 95% before they hold the perfect rotation. Read your KPIs the same way before you hand them to an agent.
      2. Simulate the agents together, not one at a time. Each sandbox agent passes any single-agent test you could write. The failure only appears when all of them run.
      3. Test for stability, not just the optimum. A plan everyone can improve on by defecting is a forecast, not a strategy.
      4. Treat the interface as part of the system. Players asked where Save was and whether all stalls were one team; students later said the chat and the controls weren’t visible together. Coordination that the screen makes hard won’t happen.
      5. Evaluate the human + agent + workflow configuration. A meta-analysis of 106 experiments found human–AI combinations on average did worse than the better of the two alone, with big differences by task. Which configuration wins is an empirical question, task by task, and Bhavya’s outage says to test the configuration without the agent too.

      There is a mirror-image risk worth knowing. In simulated markets, independent pricing algorithms have learned to keep prices high without ever communicating. Interacting agents can fail into a price war or into tacit collusion, and from the customer’s side the second is the worse failure. Either way it is the interaction, not the agent, that needs testing.

      “Do give this a shot,” Anand said. “Apply this across your business, your life. See what you learn, and publish your results.” 117:05 Which leaves the question the unrun form was going to open and close with, now answerable from the game: if every agent in your company does exactly what it is rewarded for, will the business do well? Only if someone has checked what the rewards add up to.

        Six things to take back to work

        The first session asked when to let AI act; the second, how to check it cheaply. The third ends the trilogy one level up.

        1

        Good parts can make a bad whole

        Four agents that each maximise their own mark take a food court from 100 to 79 in one round. None of them did anything a single-agent test would flag.

        2

        Rewards are the real instructions

        “60% team” paid 2.7× more for your own rupee than your neighbour’s. Convert a KPI into what it pays for before you give it to people or agents.

        3

        Effort isn’t coordination

        Workshop rooms saved 54% more plans and chatted half as much, and six of seven ended worse than they began. Activity is easy to see; convergence is what counts.

        4

        Belief isn’t use

        19 of 23 thought an agent would play better; 7 of 20 used one. Ten people believed it and didn’t reach for it. Adoption is a behaviour, not an opinion.

        5

        Optimal isn’t stable

        An agent read the code and found the perfect rotation. Every stall could still profit by breaking it. Keeping a good plan needs incentives, visibility and an owner of the whole.

        6

        Evaluate the configuration

        It is never human versus agent but human + agent + workflow versus another. Test the whole thing, including what happens when the agent is down and when the shared target stalls at twenty.
