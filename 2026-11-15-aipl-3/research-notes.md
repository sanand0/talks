## 1. Individual optimization versus system outcomes

The game is a deterministic 12-month discount/profit model, with 4 or 5 stalls, current and previous-month attraction, diminishing footfall growth, and margin erosion. The server rewards 60% collective normalized profit + 40% individual normalized profit. An important nuance: more aggressive discounting can be individually appealing without maximizing room profit; the classroom does not demonstrate that all 32 players individually optimized perfectly, nor that they used AI. Do not literally claim "every agent did its job" as an empirical finding. This is the conceptual question.

**Exact evidence:** food-court-analysis.md replays the production logs and reproduces every one of 173 matched rooms' mean-checkpoint scores, within rounding. First AIPL 88.06 versus TDS pooled 93.74; room means compared on identical normalization and checkpoint combination. Repeated AIPL sessions mix participant identities; third session all 9 use IITM-domain addresses. Keep first session and subsequent follow-ups distinct. The two cohort comparison is observational, not randomized, and game chats/Saves do not measure every collaboration channel or type of AI use.

**Useful theoretical lens:** Thomas Schelling's *Micromotives and Macrobehavior* (1978) shows why individually understandable actions may produce unintended aggregate patterns. The prisoner's dilemma is a possible teaching analogy, **not an already-proven game-theoretic classification** of this 4/5-person, 12-dimensional payoff model. The more defensible general connection is strategic externalities and mixed-motive coordination. An agent can optimize exactly what we ask and still degrade other agents' performance or the whole system. This is a reason to test interactions, incentives, temporal feedback and shared measures before scaling automation, not to give every system the same KPI regardless of task.

**Algorithmic pricing counterpoint:** Emilio Calvano, Giacomo Calzolari, Vincenzo Denicolò, Sergio Pastorello, "Artificial Intelligence, Algorithmic Pricing, and Collusion", *American Economic Review* 110(10), 2020, https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190623. In a simulated repeated price competition model, reinforcement-learning pricing algorithms learned supra-competitive prices without explicit communication. This is an *opposite* possible emergent outcome to the workshop's discount war: agent interaction may also produce undesirable tacit coordination from a consumer/competition perspective. The paper is not evidence LLM agents did this in Food Court; the agents, market models and outcomes differ. Do not recommend competitors coordinate prices. Use the workshop as a single-organization coordination problem (e.g., departments share customer outcomes while optimizing local metrics). Actual price competition can improve consumer welfare.

## 2. Do less-experienced people gain disproportionate advantage from AI?

The instructor's claim is provocative and a useful hypothesis, but stronger than the observed workshop evidence. Some professional participants thought agents/students would outperform; a minority reported using an agent. There is no measured TDS AI-use rate in these logs, nor randomized AI availability or prior experience. Participants differ in familiarity with games/coding, incentives, room size, device, cohort membership, and repeat attempts. AIPL professionals may have quite different expertise from restaurant operators, as Chumki notes. Judge actual **task performance + effort + reliability**, not credentials or tool use alone.

Independent context (optional):
- Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang, *Science* (2023), "Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence", https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586. In an experimental sample of professionals completing incentivized writing tasks, ChatGPT reduced time and increased quality, with relatively larger benefits for lower-performing workers. **Task-specific**, not proof junior accountants always beat senior ones.
- Fabrizio Dell'Acqua et al., "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier", 2023, HBS Working Paper 24-013 / https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700 (details available in paper). Experiment with BCG consultants; inside the model frontier, AI-assisted participants tended to perform better and workers below baseline average improved substantially; on a specially chosen task outside the frontier, AI users were less likely to solve correctly. The frontier is not simply "easy vs hard"; skill can include detecting the boundary. This is a particularly appropriate response to the teacher's "students + AI vs experts" hypothesis and Akshika's first-attempt objection. Don't transplant reported effect sizes into the AIPL data.
- Michelle Vaccaro, Abdullah Almaatouq and Thomas W. Malone, "When combinations of humans and AI are useful: A systematic review and meta-analysis", *Nature Human Behaviour* 8 (2024), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02024-1 . Across 106 experimental studies, combinations on average did worse than the better of human alone and AI alone; the relative result differs by task and conditions. Distinguish **human augmentation** (better than human alone) from **synergy** (better than both alternatives). This is a powerful independent support for testing the whole configuration, not assuming that adding human checks or another model always improves the strongest baseline. Historical included studies do not benchmark November 2026 frontier models.

## 3. GDPval, occupational benchmarks and "the other side has AI"

The lecturer displays an occupational AI performance visualization during his argument about expert + agent versus junior + agent. The first workshop links the author's GDPval data story https://sanand0.github.io/datastories/gdpval/; primary source OpenAI https://openai.com/index/gdpval/ (2025). GDPval assesses economically valuable tasks from 44 occupations using expert-derived deliverables; scores indicate model comparisons on sampled tasks, with evaluation and baseline conditions, **not the percentage of an occupation eliminated or verified ability to execute every task independently**. A published score can concern a model's deliverable vs a human expert's reference, not a human–agent team vs a different human–agent team. Before quoting a precise 2026 bar, inspect actual recording/snapshot and confirm model/date, rubric, task, comparator and data; do not replace the demonstrated chart with a later leaderboard. The original 2025 paper and existing Session 1 research notes are contextual evidence, not proof of current model rankings.

The screening demonstration shows the lecturer's own reported Codex success on a **practice** entrance assessment (the person visibly eats an apple while the browser agent works). Verify demonstration footage if using a numerical full-mark claim. Test proctoring does not inherently prove who made decisions; avoid identifying any real candidates, exposing exam access keys, actual exam questions, or explaining cheating workflows. Do not imply this was an official attempt or an independently audited cheating incident. The wider story is about assessment validity: what competence do we measure once work can be delegated? The practical remedy is to test decisions, process, outputs, evidence and transfer under defined tool rules.

## 4. The unrun system-failure exercises

The form was prepared with a question about sales & collections KPIs, accountability, interacting automated prices, review bottlenecks, multiple same-model verifiers, attitudes to AI vs human refund mistakes, promotion evidence, collective identical advice, review selection, capstones and a pre/post question. These are authentic *planned prompts*, not delivered data. There are 94 raw response rows but four question IDs with nonzero responses; survey-analysis.md documents counts. If useful, one or two simple thought experiments can make the systems lesson concrete:
- Throughput depends on the narrowest effective bottleneck. Faster upstream work can produce a larger queue without increasing completed work. Use explicit toy assumptions (e.g., 100 vs 20 items per hour) if making an interactive.
- Three agents using the same model need not provide three independent checks. In Session 1, the published multiple-model classification test used selected examples, with selective deferral and correlated errors. Model-count alone is no proof of independence; require agreement/disagreement coverage, test shared blind spots, and independently measure accepted-case error. See Session 1 double-checking research.
- In a repeated one-day or multi-day coordination game, many locally rational participants can pick the same apparently best option, changing the best option. No actual Diwali launch-day experiment was run here.
- For individual tasks, "what counts as correct?" is a challenge; with interacting agents, "which behavior and which system outcome is acceptable?" becomes the harder question. Define shared goals, permissions, boundaries, fallback, escalation, audit metrics and adversarial simulations. This is a **proposed operational takeaway**, not a tested capstone control.

## 6. What evidence would actually help answer the question next time?

Optional research context, not a prescribed conclusion. To isolate "agents make coordination better," randomize AI access/assistance and room-size strata, measure pre-task experience and shared-tool familiarity, standardize instruction/tutorial and incentives, assign rooms without prior pilot exposure, compare solo AI, humans without AI, and hybrid configurations under like objectives, capture external collaboration with consent, preregister room-level primary measures and power/sample calculations, and evaluate group and individual welfare, not just one normalized score. Bot-only and agent-bargaining variants could test if the rotating solution persists when objectives conflict. Don't claim to have performed such an experiment. Use the current observational workshop as motivation, not causal evidence.
