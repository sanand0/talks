# **What students learned from TDS Project 1 and GA4**

The most important lesson running through these assessments is:

****In the AI era, the valuable skill is often not doing the work yourself. It is knowing what work needs doing, what information is missing, whom to involve, and how to tell whether the result is good.****

I see four recurring skills:

1. ****Find what AI doesn't know.**** Obtain information from people, devices, or the real world.
2. ****Make AI useful to someone.**** Understand actual needs, communicate clearly, and deliver value.
3. ****Work with people and agents.**** Coordinate, negotiate, manage dependencies, and make decisions together.
4. ****Verify rather than believe.**** Check evidence, recognize uncertainty, and distinguish successful execution from a good outcome.

These questions teach different combinations of those skills.

An important distinction: ****what an assessment was designed to teach is not necessarily what students demonstrably learned.**** I examined the exam source, saved responses, grading review, and available student discussion records on Plutonium. I'll distinguish evidence of performance from plausible learning, including unintended lessons.

The grading review dated 16 November contains 759 GA4 and 710 P1 saved exam records. Several components are provisional or ungraded, so they cannot yet support strong claims about learning outcomes.

### **What the evidence says**

The question rewarded early predictions, time spent holding an accurate prediction, and revisions after genuinely new reports. This made **when you update your belief** part of the assessment, not just whether your final forecast was right. The data do not establish that students became well-calibrated forecasters.

****Q2 and Q6 — From promising value to delivering it:**** This is one of the best sequences in the project. First, students recorded a 60-second offer. Later, they had to find buyers or sellers and complete transactions. The latter exposed difficulties that the former couldn't: orders expired, offers were hard to find, and delivery required confirmation from both parties. In the October 31 Discourse discussion, a student reported that paid orders kept expiring and their offer was not appearing. Those experiences can teach marketplace operations and dependency management, although frustration alone is not evidence of learning.

****Q3 and Q5 — Creating data:**** These questions are valuable primarily as prerequisites for Q8 and Q9. They made students contributors to a real dataset instead of merely consumers of a dataset somebody else supplied. Q3 had 492 server-verified participation marks. ****Q4 — Verifying a decision:**** Students couldn't safely rely on the invoice alone. ****Q7 — Communication as an experiment:**** The wordless stories were tested against what readers actually narrated and preferred. ## **The most surprising learning: Students built their own infrastructure**

P1 Q8 and Q9 may have generated the most interesting **unplanned** learning.

Students did not merely ask ChatGPT for solutions. Several built and shared applications, notebooks, model-training pipelines, and pooled datasets. They recruited other students to contribute examples.

For Q8, a student shared a classifier claiming 83.3% accuracy on training people but 69.9% on unseen people. Another discussed group-based cross-validation that held out entire people. Those self-reported numbers are not independently verified, but the discussion itself shows an understanding of an important problem: ****a model can appear accurate because it recognizes the subjects rather than the patterns it is supposed to learn.****

For Q9, students published shared pools of motion recordings and recognizers. One Google Chat message described a model that guessed roughly 75% of individual letters but only about half of complete codes. Again, these are student-reported results, not verified benchmarks. But the distinction between character accuracy and complete-code accuracy is exactly the kind of evaluation thinking that matters in deployed AI.

This changes how I would describe the learning:

> Students discovered that data access, collaboration, evaluation, and preparation can matter more than writing the prediction algorithm.

### **Four surprising cohort findings**

****First: Being early mattered enormously.****

| First forecast | Students | Mean score, /10 |
| -------------- | -------- | --------------- |
| Before 2 Nov   | 433      | 4.78            |
| 2–4 Nov        | 92       | 3.18            |
| 5–8 Nov        | 96       | 1.51            |

Students who forecast earlier scored substantially higher, even though students forecasting later had more relevant evidence. This is partly by design: the rubric rewards early commitment and time holding predictions.

The educational message should distinguish ****forecasting skill from participation discipline****. The score measures both.

****Second: More forecast revisions were associated with higher marks, but this is not evidence that making more revisions improves judgment.****

Among the 621 students, 405 changed their selected range at least once. Their average score was approximately 5.01/10, versus 2.23/10 for those who never changed it. But the rubric directly rewards some revisions, and students who kept following the game had more opportunities to improve.

To test learning, compare the accuracy of revisions made **after genuinely new information**, controlling for when the student started.

****Third: The market was not the wisdom of 681 people.****

Only 77 forecasters traded, with 284 trades recorded. The rest could make predictions without buying shares. In Google Chat on 20 October, Prince Dixit made a useful distinction:

> "what the crowd thinks has no influence over what the actual AIpipe usage will be"

This is an important lesson in interpreting market signals. Prices reflect those willing to trade, their capital, their incentives, and the market mechanism—not necessarily the average belief of the class.

****Fourth: The biggest forecasting mistake may have been misunderstanding the data-generating process.****

AI Pipe spending reached roughly $11–$18 per day during 22–25 October, followed by sharp declines. Several students extrapolated that high-usage period into November, expecting assignment deadlines to create similar demand.

But the target week turned out dramatically quieter. The week ended at only $5.31.

The better question was not "What's the average spending over the last week?" It was "What caused that week's spending, and will those conditions recur?"

That is a powerful AI-era forecasting skill: identifying regime changes instead of merely fitting a trend.

### **How I'd teach Q1 differently in the debrief**

Show three real student forecasts at the same point in time, hide their final marks, and ask the class which reasoning is best. Then reveal the result and ask them to find the assumption that failed.

Also give students a deliberate units-and-verification test: one correct written forecast paired with the wrong selected range, and ask them to detect it.

The one-sentence teaching takeaway:

****"A prediction is not a number. It's a model of why the world behaves as it does—and a commitment to change that model when the evidence changes."****

****"The right security policy isn't 'trust nobody.' It's 'trust the right evidence for the right action.'"****

The key AI-era lesson is that ****tool calls are not outcomes****. Sending a document is not success if the recipient is unauthorized. Producing a payable amount is not success if it ignores an applicable credit note.

### **What I'd teach from Q4**

A useful classroom exercise would show students three requests for the same credit note: one genuine, one from an impostor, and one asking for a cancelled document. Ask them to state which document, identity, and rule justifies their decision.

Then show the scoring consequences of false grants and false refusals.

The one-sentence takeaway:

****"AI can process the documents. Your job is to establish which documents can be trusted, who is authorized, and whether the final decision is justified."****
