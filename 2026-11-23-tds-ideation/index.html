The rat race has a team door

The Tools in Data Science team built its next exam as a maze with more questions than anyone can finish in 45 minutes. The point: students and their agents must choose, quit, gamble and team up. The first practice logs showed something else. The questions were easy once found. The hard part was a door.

Opening Practice logs · 23 Nov

At noon, someone pressed a door 1,041 times

On Monday morning, 23 November, a practice exam went live for the Tools in Data Science (TDS) course at IIT Madras. Nobody had announced it. It was a maze: an office building seen from above, dark until you walk into it, with tasks hidden in rooms. You answer them yourself or, more likely, hand them to an AI agent.

The first student account walked in at about 11:15 am India time. Thirty-six minutes later, one account reached a heavy door that needs three people to open. It stood on a red square in front of the door and pressed “push”. And pressed. Over the next 13 minutes it pressed 1,041 times, all from the same square, in bursts of about five a second with a few pauses. The door never opened.

What happened when, and who knew

  1. 11:15 am23 NovFirst student account enters the ungraded practice maze. Logs
  2. 11:51 am–12:05One account sends 1,041 pushes at the three-person door, from one square. It doesn’t open. Logs
  3. laterthat dayOthers gather at the same door. In-game chat: “3 of 3 pushed”, “why no open?” Logs
  4. 5:30 pmIndiaThe meeting starts. 48 of the eventual 81 student accounts have already played. A student mentions the door in the first two minutes. The evidence is already in the log. Nobody has looked. Meeting
  5. 9:48 pmA student posts an experimental “maze co-pilot” in the course’s Google Chat space. After
  6. 5:25 am24 NovThe practice data is synced and analysed. Only now do the 1,041 pushes, made the previous noon, come to light. After
  7. Sun 29 NovThe real, scored maze exam. Planned

Times are India Standard Time. Counts are for accounts with IIT Madras student email addresses, which isn’t the same as unique people.

So this isn’t a scene from the next morning. It happened hours before the meeting, and the evidence was already in the log while the teachers talked. Nobody had looked. What came the next morning was only the analysis.

Don’t picture someone hammering a mouse. Bursts of five presses a second, for 13 minutes, look like a script or an agent stuck in a loop. That doesn’t make it foolish. The player did what the screen invited. The door kept asking for a push, so it got pushes. What it really needed was something different, and the screen never said so clearly.

That gap between what a screen shows and what a system checks is the thread of this story. It runs through a meeting where two teachers argue, in good faith, about what students should learn when agents can do the tasks. It runs through their exam design. And it runs, it turns out, through their own setup too.

Act I · More questions than time Meeting

“It’s a maze. Keep exploring.”

The call opens with birthday wishes for Anand, the instructor. Then Hritik, a student who is also a teaching assistant, says he has already tried the practice maze.

“I am able to solve and just one question is left, the three people pushing. Maybe I need to create three IDs and do that.”

Hritik 01:32 · “It was able to solve within, I think, 15 minutes with my rough prompt”

Anand is pleased: “which is the intent.” And he makes a note: in the real exam, logins will be limited to IIT Madras student emails, so one agent can’t log in as three people to open a three-person door 02:16. Then he explains what the maze is for. It is not a fixed paper.

“This isn’t only how do I solve the questions? It is, how do I stop and move away from a question that the agent or I are finding hard to solve? It’s about 45 minutes being the only fixed resource.”

Anand 04:04

Some moves in the game add time. There’s luck: you may roll dice to trade one thing for another. And there are other people. “Do you know your classmates’ or your group’s strengths and weaknesses well?” he asks 05:28. Later, when Preethi asks in the chat how many questions there will be, he answers with the design goal 12:00: “Make sure that there are more questions than is humanly or agentically solvable.” Time is the limit, not questions.

Two things he says need to stay attached to this. First, the practice does not count: “ROE practice will not be counted at all, for sure” 06:56. (ROE, short for Remote Online Exam, is the course’s timed, open-internet exam.) Second, finding the practice early is part of the game. Hritik had posted the link by mistake and deleted it; it was “already floating” in student groups anyway 09:16. Anand was glad.

“That’s exactly what high-frequency trading is about. You literally set up your data center next to a stock exchange, so that you can take advantage of the speed of light through the network.”

Anand, on agents that scan for new exams early 09:44 · see high-frequency trading

So the exam is meant to test choosing, not just answering. Try it. Below is a made-up floor with six tasks. Pick the ones you’d go for in 45 minutes.

Act I · More questions than time Meeting

The setting he thought was on

Anand designs these exams for agents as much as for people. Early in the call he says, in passing, that he’d “think enabled WebMCP” 01:56, so students could give their agents a skill for the game. WebMCP is an early-preview browser standard that lets a web page hand agents a menu of actions.

Nine minutes later, Hritik asks why there’s no MCP for the question. His agent is getting errors.

Hritik

“Yeah, my agent is saying that with all the known point, it is returning 404.” 11:12

Anand

“When I was designing this, I was debating WebMCP versus API, and finally decided not to go for the WebMCP, but don’t read that as a final decision. … But you’re right. No currently, there is no WebMCP.” 11:24

It’s a small moment, and he fixes his own record within ten minutes. But it is the story in miniature: the designer’s belief about the system and the system itself had drifted apart, and a student’s agent was what noticed. The maze did have an agent interface, just a different one: a plain JSON API that the browser game itself uses. Whether WebMCP gets added later was left open. (The WebMCP draft spec is still changing, and it isn’t something every phone or browser supports yet.)

Act I · More questions than time Meeting

“Make sure that the other rats collaborate”

Then JK, who joined a few minutes earlier, sees where unlimited questions lead.

“If you want to win the rat race, we make sure that the other rats collaborate with us. You understood where I’m getting at, right?”

JK (Jayakrishnan Warriem) 12:36 · laughter

Anand understood. If the questions never run out, there’s no fixed maximum, so marks get scaled to the best score in the class. He’d said it bluntly at the start: “if there’s somebody who does really well, they can totally screw it up for everyone else. But that’s the nature of unbounded opportunities” 03:36. Now he spells out the incentive.

“The person who scores the highest mark is totally going to mess up everybody’s scores because that is going to be the denominator. So, are you able to collaborate so that somebody doesn’t get too far ahead?”

Anand 12:56 · “all of the classic symptoms of relative grading now start coming in”

Your work doesn’t change. Your mark does.

Hypothetical scoring

Suppose marks are your raw score divided by the top raw score. You scored 24. The class median is 18. Drag the top scorer.

At a top score of 30, you get 24 / 30 = 80%. If one classmate reaches 60, you get 24 / 60 = 40% for the same work.

This was a proposal discussed in the meeting, not announced policy, and there’s no evidence anyone sabotaged anyone. The scored exam’s formula may differ. But the arithmetic shows why JK laughed. Under this rule, a brilliant classmate who shares a solver with the class helps everyone; one who races ahead alone lowers everyone else’s mark. Helping and competing get tangled. Anand expects students to take a while to notice: “cooperativeness is the default, which is nice. And I think it’ll take a few batches for the competitiveness to also set in” 13:52.

Act II · The class builds a swarm Meeting

Builders, testers, and nobody keeping the list

JK has been reading the students’ group chats. What surprises him is how fast help appears. Someone describes a problem, and “suddenly, ‘Yeah, this solver should help you’” 14:28. He’d rather this happened on the course forum, “where at least I know who these people are” (laughter), but he likes it: “this is what we wanted right from the start.” Then he sketches who does what.

“If you look at the number of people who are putting out solvers consistently, I can clearly see in the groups there are like two or three people. … And there is a silent, passive majority.”

JK 16:20

Anand maps it onto open-source software. There are the people who write the code. There are the people who keep a community running: triaging bug reports, merging fixes. And that second role, he says, is missing.

“I would imagine there would be a tracker that says, ‘Here are the dates. Here are the questions … here are the solvers. Here is the reported success rate of each of the solvers, and here are the known gaps.’ … That organization layer has not yet evolved.”

Anand 17:44

JK sees why the role matters beyond this course. In a job interview, he says, the best answer may soon be “In a swarm of humans and agents, this was my role” 18:28. Knowing what you’re good at inside a team of people and machines “cracked the code of, ‘Okay, how do you make yourself more relevant in the age of AI?’”

Meanwhile, inside the maze Practice logs · 23 Nov

Players can leave short notes on the floor for whoever walks by next. The maze recorded 94 notes from student accounts by the next morning. They read like a community writing its own manual:

do NOT go 1 step south from this square, it is a trap Its a Dead end stay here i will bring other just stay there are 2 tasks behind it nothing else anywhere push the team door in 1 2 3

Anonymous, as written. Player names and locations removed.

And that night, after the meeting, a student posted an experimental “maze co-pilot” in the course’s Google Chat space After · 23 Nov. As described in the post, it explores the dark map while avoiding known traps, ranks tasks by distance and marks, and lets classmates swap the maps they’ve uncovered. Its heuristic: tasks many others have solved are probably quick wins. That’s a maintainer-shaped tool: shared maps are the tracker Anand was asking for. Nobody has tested how well it works, so this page doesn’t link it.

Act II · The class builds a swarm Meeting

“What is it that I learned is more important”

JK wants the class to share more than solvers. He’d like students to share their agent logs, the full back-and-forth with the AI, in a common repository 21:24. He imagines a later assignment where students code such a log: which parts of the thinking did the human do, and which did the AI? Anand hears two ideas, “sharing the logs; second, identifying the component of contribution.” Which one matters?

“What is it that I learned is more important, because the moment I realize that I learned this thing, the next turn when I do, I am going to contribute more. That’s my hypothesis.”

JK 24:08

Anand shares his screen. Two days earlier he’d run a computer-vision workshop at SUTD in Singapore and asked one question at the end: what did you learn? Answers included “AI can create a website and a game, but I didn’t know it could do so much,” and “Importance of prompting versus coding,” the second from a student who coded well 26:08.

That’s a record of what students felt they learned, not proof they learned it. Anand suggests starting small: after a single exam question, ask “What have you learned by trying this question?” 27:20, and accept honest answers like “Wait for somebody to come up with a solver.” None of this has been built yet. Shared agent logs would also need consent and redaction first: they can hold private files, keys, and other people’s data.

Act III · Capture before you organize Meeting

“You blink or your agent blinks, and it loses”

JK explains how he works now. Teams should keep everything in one shared place, with versions, like Git, and permissions by role. Learning to organize files and information was, for him, one of the biggest lessons in computing, and no course teaches it.

“As humans now, while working with AI, all I am now doing is putting files in appropriate folders, ensuring that the permission levels are correct.”

JK 29:20 · laughter

Anand starts designing a question out loud. People carry jigsaw pieces into a room; only you can move yours; the room wins together or not at all. Then he catches himself: “that doesn’t necessarily teach organization, this teaches coordination” 30:44. A minute later he turns JK’s point around.

“Don’t lose any information, rather than make sure you organize it correctly, being the more valuable skill. … There is an error that appears transiently, I wasn’t watching it, or I didn’t log it, gone. Now there is no retrieval.”

Anand 32:12 · “Organization is secondary, and organization is an intelligence problem that is solvable.”

His argument: a careless pile in a shared drive can be sorted later by an agent, today or next year. A signal that was never captured can’t be. So maybe the exam should test event capture: a page where things go wrong briefly, “clicks that do not get transmitted, console errors,” and you have to notice. JK names it at once: “Exactly. Observability” 34:12, the engineering practice of making a system report what it’s doing (see Google’s SRE chapter on monitoring and the OpenTelemetry primer). Then Anand gives it a shape a game can use: “Get data in one room, answer questions in the next room, and you can’t go back” 34:56.

The game itself proves his point. The only reason this page can tell you about the 1,041 pushes is that the maze wrote down every event, raw, with a timestamp: 160,489 of them across all accounts on the first day. Nobody organized them as they happened. The analysis the next morning was the “organize later” step, and it worked because nothing had been thrown away.

Act III · Capture before you organize Meeting

“This is my fundamental problem with that thesis”

Anand goes further: “I’m also increasingly of the opinion that organization is mattering less and less” 35:24. Hritik has compared RAG, GraphRAG and agentic search, he notes, and whatever wins is true “only today.” JK doesn’t accept it.

“This is my fundamental problem with that thesis. Because this organization is a skill that is … taught as a transferable skill, right? You come to a clumsy into a chaos, you should be able to identify order inside it.”

JK 36:04 · organizing first means “the search space is … shrunk significantly”

Anand agrees, partly. The question determines how you organize. Hand an agent the question and it can often work out the structure. What’s scarce is knowing which question is worth asking.

“Knowing that, ‘Here is an interesting or an important question that can be answered from this data,’ is, I think, a growing skill.”

Anand 37:40

His example is JK’s own: look at the student chats and ask who sits at the center of the network and who benefits quietly. That’s turning a mess into a useful question. JK concedes, with a joke that is also good method: “I have to always put my hypothesis as on so-and-so date and time, timestamp it” 39:08, because what holds today may not hold next month.

Act IV · What the log already knew After · analysis of 24 Nov

The questions were easy once you found them

Midway through the call, Anand reads a number off his screen: about 26 students had submitted, 20 with full marks on the practice page 19:24. “The majority managed to solve it once discovered in a few hours, which is the straightforward part.” That was the exam page’s count at that moment. The next morning’s analysis counted something different, from the maze’s own logs, over a longer window. Keep the two apart.

By 7:55 am Singapore time on 24 November, 81 accounts with IIT Madras student emails had entered the practice maze. (Another 11 accounts, staff and personal emails, are left out.) Here’s what they scored. These are raw practice marks, not grades: the practice counts for nothing.

The design wanted more tasks than anyone could finish. In this first practice, five accounts finished them all, and 20 got close. For strong players, the limit wasn’t time. It was supply. (Making the real maze “ever-expanding” is on the designer’s to-do list. It isn’t built yet.)

The next chart is the clearest finding of the morning.

Read the bars from the side, not the end. Ten open-floor tasks were each attempted by 42 to 55 accounts. The room behind a locked door: 24. The room behind the three-person door: 10. Once there, players almost always solved the task. What varied was whether they got there. Agents had made the questions easy. The maze had moved the difficulty into reaching them: walking, luck with keys, and other people.

Act IV · What the log already knew Pressed 23 Nov, before the meeting Found 24 Nov

The door counted clicks, not readiness

Back to the door. Remember the timing: most of these pushes, including all 1,041 from one account, were made before the meeting began. They sat in the log through the whole call, including the moment Hritik mentioned the three-person door. The analysis surfaced them the next morning. Here is everything the logs say.

1,832pushes at the three-person door from student accounts
42student accounts that pushed at least once
1,041from one account, one square, 13 minutes; never opened
11student accounts that got through

What players said at the door In-game chat · 23 Nov

push the door we need 1 more person why it shows 2 of 3 3 of 3 pushed why no open? i am orangepurple? all take a red square we have to do it together

Anonymous, as written; not consecutive. Notes left nearby add “pushed sooo many times”.

This is real coordination. Strangers recruited each other, assigned squares by color, counted down. And sometimes the screen said three of three while the door stayed shut.

Why? The analysis checked the game’s code. To open, the door needs players on enough different squares at about the same time. But the number it shows players counts people who pushed. Two people on one square plus a third on another: the display says “3 of 3”; the door, checking squares, sees two. The display reported one thing. The lock checked another.

The model below is generic. It’s not the game’s code or map. It shows only the mismatch.

What does “3 of 3” measure?

As set up: three players pushed, so the screen says 3 of 3. But two share square 2, so the lock sees two squares. The door stays shut.

Now reread the 1,041 pushes. One account, one square, alone. It could never open the door alone; no amount of pushing would. But nothing told it that the missing ingredient was other squares, not more pushes. An agent told to “push until it opens” would do exactly what the logs show. The test reported the wrong measure for the state it required.

That’s not a scandal. It’s why there’s a practice. The analysis suggested a fix: let each player mark themselves ready on a square, keep it lit while they stand there, and open when enough squares are lit. It also flagged that players who arrived later in the day found fewer people around, a pattern consistent with presence mattering, though early players may simply have been keener. (Whether the fix ships before Sunday wasn’t known when this was written. This page deliberately leaves out the exact rules of the real door.)

The exam tries to measure how students orchestrate agents and people under uncertainty. Its first practice may also have measured whether they could reach a task, find two people at the same moment, and read a misleading display.

Act V · What remains Meeting

A month of decisions, in a day

Back in the meeting, JK asks the question under all of this: “What should we teach them?” 39:36. He has a worry from closed-book quizzes, where students have no internet and no AI. In a Python course, he says, pass rates that used to be 70–80% have dropped to about 50%, on similar questions 40:04. That’s his reading of his own data, not a published study, but the concern is real: is the ability to think without help fading?

“My philosophy is: let the agent do what it does well, what it cannot do is what we need to learn. And that is shrinking, so at the very least, we have to operate at the current boundary and beyond.”

Anand 40:56

What’s left at the boundary, he says, is not a list of technical skills. It’s intuition, creative spark (in specifying and in verifying), skepticism, and taste 42:12. Quizzes may help less “until people have a chance to build that muscle in the first place. Use an agent like crazy.”

JK brings an argument he heard from people wary of tech in classrooms. If agents automate most routine work, what’s left for humans is the hard, emotional part: the support call that has already gone wrong, the irate customer. That drains people fast. Anand has seen a version of it in himself.

“Now, the agents are executing for us, so you’re just deciding, deciding, deciding, deciding. Compressing a month’s worth of decisions into a day.”

Anand 46:04 · “the mistakes also multiply … And when you’re tired, even more so.”

His conclusion is the opposite of what you might expect: “That’s an argument to make TDS tougher, not easier” 47:00. Decision fatigue here is a lived hypothesis from two teachers, not settled science. But it reframes the maze. Forty-five minutes of choosing what to skip, whom to trust and when to quit is a rehearsal for that kind of day, if the choices it presents are the real ones.

Act V · What remains After

Was it judgment, or just the maze?

Here is the strongest objection to everything above. The practice maze was meant to show students making smart human-and-agent decisions under pressure. Maybe what it showed was game friction, self-selection and luck. The evidence cuts both ways.

It may have measured the maze

The early players found an unannounced link: 48 of the 81 accounts played before the meeting even started. Tasks were deliberately easy and the practice was ungraded. The door’s display was misleading. Players who came later may have found nobody to team up with. 18 accounts scored zero, for reasons the data can’t tell.

It also showed real judgment

Students wrote warnings and recruited strangers with notes, assigned squares by color, and counted down. A student built a co-pilot that ranks tasks and shares maps. The five who reached the ceiling, and the 20 at 23+, got there by choosing and navigating, because the solving was easy.

Both are true, which is the point of a practice. The test needed testing, and it got tested: by a student’s agent that hit 404s on a missing interface, by players who saw “3 of 3” and a shut door, by a log that kept every push. That is the series’ recurring theme, now built into a game: the exam has to pass its own exam.

So the question the meeting couldn’t answer is the one to carry into Sunday. Can a score tell genuine judgment — what to skip, whom to trust, when to quit — apart from navigation, luck with keys, who happened to be online, and a confusing display? If the door were replaced with a clear, persistent handshake, would the scores still separate good collaborators from the rest? Nobody knows yet. That’s worth measuring.

    When agents solve the questions, test the test

    Six things to carry out of this meeting and its first morning of data.

    1

    Check what the display counts

    The door showed pushes; the lock checked squares. Any score, dashboard or progress bar can report a different thing from what it requires.

    2

    Agents moved the difficulty

    503 of 520 practice attempts were solved. The hard part was reaching tasks, getting a key, and finding two people at once.

    3

    Relative marks make classmates variables

    Divide by the top score and one runaway score halves everyone else’s. Decide whether that is the skill you mean to reward.

    4

    Capture first, organize later

    An agent can sort a pile; it can’t recover what was never recorded. The door is visible only because every push was logged.

    5

    Communities need maintainers

    Builders and users appear on their own. The tracker of tasks, versions and known failures doesn’t, until someone takes the role.

    6

    Measure judgment apart from friction

    Choosing, trusting and quitting well is the goal. Navigation, luck, timing and a confusing screen are noise to remove before Sunday.
